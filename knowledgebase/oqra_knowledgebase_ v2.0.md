# **Global Definitions**

**Core Rubric & Audit Frameworks:**

* **Green Rubric**: Standards that establish foundational quality expectations and core requirements for online and hybrid course design.  
* **Gold Rubric**: Standards that represent advanced, exemplary instructional design practices aimed at enhancing active learning, community building, and real-world application.  
* **Course-Level Learning Outcomes**: Broad statements describing the overarching knowledge, skills, or competencies learners will achieve upon completing the entire course.  
* **Module-Level Learning Outcomes**: Specific, measurable objectives describing learner proficiency in discrete components to be demonstrated at regular intervals throughout the course.  
* **Alignment**: The critical structural relationship ensuring course outcomes, assessments, instructional materials, learning activities, and tools work cohesively to support learner achievement.  
* **Specific Review Standard**: A discrete, evaluation standard used to audit specific course design components.  
* **Passing Threshold**: The explicit quantitative or frequency-based benchmark (e.g., 85% compliance or minimum 1 instance) required for a course component to satisfy a Specific Review Standard. 

**Course Architecture & Learning Outcomes:**

* **Alignment**: The critical structural relationship ensuring course outcomes, assessments, instructional materials, learning activities, and tools work cohesively to support learner achievement.  
* **Measurable Verbs**: Action-oriented verbs (e.g., *analyze, apply, create, explain*) that describe observable performance tasks capable of direct evaluation, avoiding passive mental behaviors like *understand* or *know*.  
* **Course Map**: A visual diagram, matrix, or structural alignment document outlining how learning outcomes directly connect to assigned learning activities, instructional materials, and assessments. 

**Interaction, Presence, & Community:**

* Regular and Substantive Interaction (RSI): An instructor’s stated plan for predictable, ongoing communication and direct instruction, defining specific response intervals for inquiries and substantive feedback on coursework.  
* Social Presence: The ability of online participants to project their personal characteristics into the learning community, presenting themselves as real, approachable people to foster trust and group cohesion.  
* Teaching Presence: The intentional design, active facilitation, and direct instruction applied by the instructor to guide social and cognitive learning processes toward meaningful outcomes.  
* Netiquette: Explicitly stated community and communication guidelines governing professional, respectful, and culturally inclusive interactions across written, oral, and visual channels**.**

**Course Technology, Accessibility, & Usability:** 

* **Voluntary Product Accessibility Template (VPAT) / Accessibility Statement**: Official documentation provided by software vendors detailing the accessibility compliance features, standards, and technical limitations of digital tools. 

# **General Standard 1**

* **Category**: Course Overview and Introduction  
* **General Standard Summary**: The overall design of the course is made clear to the learner at the beginning of the course.  
* **General Standard Overview**: The course overview and introduction set the tone for the course, let learners know what to expect, and provide other guidance to help learners succeed from the beginning.

## **Standard 1.1**

* **ID**: GR-S1.1  
* **Category**: Course Overview and Introduction  
* **Summary**: Instructions make clear how to get started and where to find various course components.  
* **Rubric Type**: Green  
* **Points**: 3pts  
* **Priority Weight**: High  
* **Scope of Implementation**: Course home page, "Start Here" / "Read Me First" orientation module, Course Overview page, introductory video/tour, syllabus, and initial welcome announcements.  
* **Passing Threshold**: Minimum 1 instance providing clear "getting started" instructions, course orientation, and navigational guidance on the course home page or orientation module at the beginning of the course.  
* **Annotation**: Information posted at the beginning of the course indicates what to do first, provides a general course overview and detailed navigational instructions, and encourages the learner to explore the course site. Instructors may choose to incorporate some of this information in the course syllabus or other course documents. A useful feature is a “Read Me First” or “Start Here” button or icon on the course home page, linking learners to introductory information.   
* **Rationales**: Providing explicit instructions on how to get started and where to locate essential course components reduces initial anxiety and eliminates technical navigation barriers for learners during their first visit. Setting clear expectations early, providing a comprehensive course overview, and encouraging self-directed site exploration enables learners to navigate the course with ease, manage their study time effectively, and focus immediately on academic content.   
* **Note to Reviewers**: Use the first visit to the course site to experience what learners encounter when they log in for the first time. Look for clear directions on what to do first, where to find the syllabus or orientation section, and how to navigate the course layout. Determine whether learners are given sufficient information to begin the course with ease. Check for prominent orientation links (such as a "Start Here" or "Read Me First" button/icon on the home page), visual course maps, video walkthroughs, or interactive scavenger hunts/syllabus quizzes that prompt students to explore the site.   
* **Hybrid Courses**: Instructions in the online course site make it clear to learners that the course is a hybrid course, with both online and face-to-face components and activities. Instructions specify the format, sequencing, and requirements for both the online and face-to-face portions of the course.   
* **Competency-Based Courses**: N/A  
* **Synchronous Courses**: Information is provided in the asynchronous course site about where to find various course components and guides learners on how and when to connect to synchronous class sessions.   
* **Aligned Elements**:  
  * **Getting Started & Orientation Anchors**:  
    * A prominent **"Start Here"** or **"Read Me First"** button, icon, or link located directly on the course home page.  
    * A clearly stated, step-by-step explanation detailing what to do first to begin the course.  
    * Essential introductory guidance provided on a dedicated **Course Overview page**, a standalone orientation page, or within a "Getting Started" module template.  
  * **Course Overview & Navigational Guidance**:  
    * A comprehensive "Course Overview" page outlining key syllabus components, course structure, and overarching expectations.   
    * A general course overview explaining the overall design, structure, and foundational expectations of the course.  
    * Detailed navigational instructions that guide learners on where to find various course components, syllabus documents, and orientation materials.  
    * A recorded **course tour** or **virtual walkthrough** utilizing screen recordings and screenshots to demonstrate course navigation.  
    * Visual representations of the course structure, such as illustrations, tables, diagrams, or graphic maps.  
  * **Exploration & Interactive Activities**:  
    * A **scavenger hunt** or **syllabus quiz** assignment designed to guide learners through exploring different sections of the course site.  
    * A welcoming course introduction video that explicitly covers starting steps, navigational guidance, and encouragement to explore the site.  
    * Automated reminders or course announcements (email) encouraging learners to navigate and explore course resources.  
    * Interactive video quizzes focused on orientation information and navigation.  
  * **Modality-Specific Navigation & Instructions**:  
    * **Hybrid Courses**: Explicit instructions detailing the format, sequencing, and requirements for both online and face-to-face portions of the course.  
    * **Synchronous Courses**: Clear guides within the asynchronous site explaining where to find components and how/when to connect to live synchronous class sessions.  
* **Violating Examples**:  
  * The course home page opens to a plain list of module folders without any "Start Here" banner, welcome announcement, or instructions explaining what students should do first.  
  * A course syllabus lists assignments and readings, but the LMS course site provides no navigation guide, video tour, or explanation showing students where to locate those items in the system.  
  * In a hybrid course, students log into the LMS but find no orientation text explaining how the online modules integrate with face-to-face meetings or which tasks must be completed prior to the first in-person session.  
  * In a synchronous course, the course site lacks instructions or direct links informing students where to access live web sessions or how real-time meetings align with asynchronous course materials.

## **Standard 1.2**

* **ID**: GR-S1.2  
* **Category**: Course Overview and Introduction  
* **Summary**: Learners are introduced to the purpose and structure of the course.  
* **Rubric Type**: Green  
* **Points**: 3pts  
* **Priority Weight**: High  
* **Scope of Implementation**: Course syllabus, course orientation / "Start Here" module, Course Overview page, course introduction/tour video, and course schedule/calendar.  
* **Passing Threshold**: 100% compliance across both core components—learners must be introduced to both (1) the purpose of the course AND (2) the structure of the course (how the learning process is carried out, weekly schedule, activity types, and assessment methods) on the course site at the start of the course.  
* **Annotation**: Information is provided in the course site to help learners understand the purpose of the course and how the learning process is structured and carried out, including a course schedule, an explanation of the delivery modality (e.g., online or hybrid), modes of communication and participation, types of learning activities, and how learning will be assessed. Information may also include explanations of the purpose of specific course activities, such as to build community, to expand perspectives, or to foster inclusivity and belonging.   
* **Rationales**: Introducing learners to both the overarching purpose and operational structure of a course establishes cognitive clarity, sets clear behavioral expectations, and reduces learner anxiety at the start of the term. Explaining why the course matters within the discipline/curriculum and detailing how weekly activities, communication modes, delivery modalities, and assessments operate helps students navigate the LMS efficiently, manage their time effectively, and actively engage in the learning process.   
* **Note to Reviewers**: Look in the course syllabus, Course Overview page, orientation module, or course introduction video for information that clearly introduces BOTH the purpose and the structure of the course. If BOTH the purpose and the structure are explained, mark this Specific Review Standard as "Met." Ensure that the purpose addresses the course's role within the major/curriculum, its relevance to learners' lives, or targeted competencies (e.g., community building, perspective expansion, inclusivity). Ensure that the structure covers the schedule/calendar, delivery modality, typical weekly rhythm, learning activity types, communication/participation modes, and assessment framework.   
* **Hybrid Courses**: The purpose of both the online and face-to-face portions of the course is clearly explained to learners to help them understand how and why both formats are important to the learning process. The course schedule or calendar fully covers both the online and face-to-face portions of the course and clearly specifies the dates, times, locations, and content of face-to-face class meetings.   
* **Competency-Based Courses**: In addition to the purpose of the competency-based course, the structure of the course and the options available to learners to complete the course are clearly delineated through detailed visuals and/or instructions. Reviewers may look for this information in the course site or linked from the course site to the program website.   
* **Synchronous Courses**: Information is provided to help learners understand what will occur during synchronous class sessions, such as discussions, lectures, etc., and what will be in the asynchronous course site, such as additional assessments, instructional materials, etc. The course schedule or calendar fully covers both the synchronous and asynchronous portions of the course and clearly specifies the dates, times, locations, and content of synchronous class meetings.   
* **Aligned Elements**:  
  * **Course Purpose & Relevance Elements**:  
    * An explicit statement outlining the purpose of the course within the major, broader curriculum, or discipline.  
    * An explanation detailing how the course contributes to the learner’s overall academic or professional education.  
    * A description highlighting how the course content is relevant to learners' lives, careers, and practical applications beyond the course.  
    * Explicit rationale explaining how course activities build community, expand perspectives, and foster inclusivity and belonging.  
    * Purpose statements embedded prominently on the **Course Overview page** or featured within a course introduction video.  
  * **Course Structure & Workflow Elements**:  
    * A course outline, alignment map, or structural overview page explaining how the learning process is structured and carried out.  
    * A **"typical week" schedule** or weekly workflow outline detailing the standard set of activities students must complete each topic or week.  
    * A recorded course tour or walkthrough depicting the course structure and typical weekly module layout.  
    * Clear descriptions of expected modes of communication, interaction, and student participation.  
  * **Schedule, Delivery, & Assessment Elements**:  
    * A comprehensive course schedule, calendar grid, or activity listing detailing dates, topics, and deadlines.  
    * An explanation of the delivery modality (e.g., online, hybrid, synchronous) and why that format fits the course design.  
    * An overview statement outlining the types of learning activities used in the course and explaining how student learning will be evaluated and assessed  
  * **Modality-Specific Purpose & Structure Elements**:  
    * **Hybrid Courses**: Clear explanations of why both online and face-to-face formats are essential to the learning process, supported by a unified course calendar specifying meeting dates, times, physical locations, and session content.  
    * **Synchronous Courses**: An explicit breakdown detailing what occurs during real-time synchronous sessions (e.g., live discussions, lectures) versus asynchronous modules (e.g., assessments, readings), accompanied by exact dates, times, and access details for live meetings.  
    * **Competency-Based Courses**: Detailed visual diagrams, flowcharts, or step-by-step instructions (located in the course site or linked program page) delineating course structure and available options for completing competencies.  
* **Violating Examples**:  
  * A course provides a detailed weekly reading and assignment schedule but includes zero explanation of the course purpose, its role in the curriculum, or why the subject matter is relevant to students.  
  * The syllabus lists the course description from the university catalog, but provides no information explaining how the online/hybrid structure works, what a typical weekly schedule looks like, or how learning activities and assessments are conducted.  
  * In a hybrid or synchronous course, the course calendar lists asynchronous assignment due dates but fails to provide dates, meeting times, locations, or content breakdown for mandatory face-to-face or live virtual sessions.

## **Standard 1.3**

* **ID**: GR-S1.3  
* **Category**: Course Overview and Introduction  
* **Summary**: Communication guidelines for the course are clearly stated.  
* **Rubric Type**: Green  
* **Points**: 2pts  
* **Priority Weight**: Medium  
* **Scope of Implementation**: Course syllabus, course orientation / "Start Here" module, dedicated Community/Communication Guidelines page, Course Overview page, policy section, orientation video, or social contract creation activity.  
* **Passing Threshold**: Minimum 1 instance clearly outlining explicit communication and netiquette expectations on the course site at the beginning of the course (unless learner interaction is deemed not appropriate on the Course Worksheet for continuing education courses).  
* **Annotation**: Guidelines for communication (sometimes referred to as “community guidelines”) are clearly stated in the course syllabus or other course documents. Forms of communication may include written, oral, and/or visual. Communication methods may include email, discussion forums, assignments, synchronous course sessions, and other interactions or collaborations. As behavior within the learning community is culturally and contextually influenced, it is important to be explicit about expectations for communication within the course. Learners may be invited to contribute to forming the course’s social contract or community guidelines.  
* **Rationales**: Establishing explicit communication and community guidelines at the beginning of a course defines professional netiquette and behavioral expectations across written, oral, and visual channels. Clear standards foster a respectful, culturally inclusive, and psychologically safe environment for collaborative learning, reducing interpersonal friction and ensuring constructive academic dialogue.   
* **Note to Reviewers**: Look in the syllabus or other course documents for clearly stated communication guidelines across written, oral, visual, email, forum, assignment, and synchronous session channels. Note that the substance of the guidelines is NOT evaluated and all types of communication may not be applicable to every course. Reviewers can look for links or references to student conduct codes, explicit examples of unacceptable behaviors (e.g., cyberbullying, slurs, microaggressions), or collaborative social contract activities. Continuing Education Courses: Check the Course Worksheet to determine if learner interaction is appropriate; if interaction among learners is not appropriate, mark this Specific Review Standard as "Met."   
* **Hybrid Courses**: Information is available on the course site to inform learners if there are unique, additional policies or requirements for real-time communication. Information might include guidance about addressing peers, engaging in respectful spoken discussions, and any requirements for being on camera during synchronous or hybrid meetings.   
* **Competency-Based Courses**: If there are unique, additional policies for this modality, they are included on the course site.   
* **Synchronous Courses**: Information is available on the course site to inform learners if there are unique, additional policies or requirements for real-time communication. Information might include guidance about addressing peers, engaging in respectful spoken discussions, and any requirements for being on camera in a synchronous course.   
* **Aligned Elements**:  
  * **Written & Netiquette Standards**:  
    * Explicit expectations requiring respectful tone across email, discussion boards, peer reviews, and digital messaging.  
    * Guidance regarding written mechanics, such as using the official language of instruction, avoiding "netspeak" or internet abbreviations, and maintaining appropriate academic/professional conventions.  
  * **Inclusive & Respectful Verbal/Visual Communication**:  
    * Guidelines acknowledging that oral communication may reflect regional dialects or culturally embedded ways of communicating (e.g., AAVE or Castilian Spanish) while maintaining mutual respect.  
    * Expectations for respectful video interactions, including appropriate verbal communication, non-verbal cues, and body language.  
  * **Unacceptable Behaviors & Conduct References**:  
    * Clear definitions of unacceptable conduct (e.g., bullying, microaggressions, use of slurs) and explicit statements detailing how violations will be addressed.  
    * Links or references to the institutional student handbook or code of conduct to reinforce community standards (aligned with Standard 5.4).  
  * **Real-Time & Camera Expectations**:  
    * Specific guidelines for live virtual meetings, including peer addressing norms, microphone management, respectful spoken debate, and camera expectations.  
  * **Interactive & Prominent Presentation**:  
    * Communication guidelines embedded on a dedicated LMS page, within a policy section, highlighted in an introduction video, or co-created with students via an orientation social contract activity.  
  * **Modality-Specific Communication Criteria:**  
    * **Hybrid and Synchronous Courses:** Clear, published policies for real-time interactions, including proper guidance on addressing peers, conducting respectful live spoken discussions, and explicit requirements for camera and microphone usage during synchronous class meetings.  
    * **Competency-Based Courses:** Specialized communication guidelines detailed on the course site addressing unique modality interaction expectations.  
* **Violating Examples**:  
  * The course relies heavily on weekly discussion forums and peer reviews, but provides no written communication guidelines or netiquette rules anywhere in the syllabus or course site.  
  * In a synchronous online course with mandatory webcam attendance, the course site provides zero guidance regarding camera etiquette, speaking norms, or respectful live chat communication.  
  * The syllabus states "be respectful online" without detailing basic expectations for academic communication, netiquette, or how inappropriate conduct (e.g., harassment or microaggressions) is handled.

## **Standard 1.4**

* **ID**: GR-S1.4  
* **Category**: Course Overview and Introduction  
* **Summary**: Course and institutional policies with which the learner is expected to comply are clearly stated, or links are provided.  
* **Rubric Type**: Green  
* **Points**: 2pts  
* **Priority Weight**: Medium  
* **Scope of Implementation**: Course syllabus, course orientation / "Start Here" module, dedicated Course & Institutional Policies page, and student support resources section on the course site.  
* **Passing Threshold**: Minimum 1 instance clearly stating or directly linking to current institutional and course-specific policies on the course site at the beginning of the course.  
* **Annotation**: The number and type of policies vary by instructor or institution. Look for links to the student handbook or other institution-wide policy publications and determine that all policies that apply to learners are stated or linked in the course. Check the Course Worksheet to determine whether policies are available to learners through an institutional portal rather than within the course. Course and institutional policies may address such matters as student conduct, academic integrity, establishing attendance, the grade of “Incomplete,” withdrawal without penalty, confidentiality in the classroom, religious accommodations, provisions for military service members and veterans, student grievances, electronic communication, proctoring, accessibility (see also Specific Review Standard 7.2), and others. Confirm that the policies are clearly explained and the policy statements themselves, or a direct link to them, are prominently located in the course.   
* **Rationales**: Clearly stating or directly linking to course and institutional policies establishes transparent operational boundaries, legal compliance, and behavioral expectations for learners from the start of the course. Informing students upfront about academic integrity, grading policies, attendance requirements, accessibility, and student rights minimizes misunderstandings, promotes academic honesty, and ensures equitable treatment across all learning scenarios.   
* **Note to Reviewers**: Look in the course syllabus or other course documents for information that clearly states or links to course and institutional policies. Check the Course Worksheet to verify if policies are provided via an institutional portal rather than within the course site (use uploaded policies from the Course Worksheet if behind a password-protected portal). Note that the substance and quantity of policies are NOT evaluated and do not determine whether Standard 1.4 is met. Ensure essential course policies like late work and academic integrity are included (suggest their addition if missing, referencing Standards 3.2 and 3.6).   
* **Hybrid Courses**: If there are unique, additional policies for real-time or face-to-face class sessions, they are included on the course site.   
* **Competency-Based Courses**: If there are unique, additional policies for this modality, they are included on the course site.   
* **Synchronous Courses**: If there are unique, additional policies for live virtual or real-time class sessions, they are included on the course site.   
* **Aligned Elements**:  
  * **Institutional Policies (Direct Statements or Direct Links)**:   
    * Direct working links to the university's central policy publications, student handbook, or institutional policy portal.  
    * Statements or links covering official institutional policies on student conduct and academic integrity.  
    * Grading policies including the grade of "Incomplete" and "Withdrawal without penalty."   
    * Explicit links or statements addressing student accessibility and disability accommodation policies (aligned with Standard 7.2).  
    * Statements outlining provisions for religious accommodations, military service members, and veterans.  
    * Formal guidelines and direct links regarding institutional student grievance procedures.  
  * **Instructor & Course-Level Specific Policies**:  
    * An explicit late work policy stating exact point deduction percentages, grace periods, or rules for case-by-case late submission review (aligned with Standard 3.2).  
    * Detailed academic integrity policy contextualized to the course, including clear definitions of what constitutes plagiarism or unauthorized collaboration.  
    * Course-level attendance and participation policies (e.g., establishing first-day attendance and ongoing participation expectations).   
    * Guidelines for electronic communication and email response time expectations.   
    * Explicit rules and technical expectations regarding online exam proctoring services.  
    * Confidentiality expectations for classroom discussions and peer sharing.   
    * Proctoring rules, expectations, and testing environment requirements.  
  * **Location, Visibility, & Presentation Anchors**:  
    * Policy statements integrated directly into the syllabus or featured on a dedicated "Course Policies" page within the orientation module.  
    * Direct, prominent placement of policy text or un-broken direct hyperlinks so learners can access them immediately at the start of the course.  
    * Clear sample texts or direct references provided in course orientation materials to ensure policies are easy to locate and understand.  
  * **Modality-Specific Policy Criteria**:  
    * **Hybrid and Synchronous Courses**: Explicit, published policies governing real-time class sessions, including synchronous attendance requirements, live participation rules, and camera/recording guidelines.  
    * **Competency-Based Courses**: Modality-specific course policies integrated into the course site to govern flexible competency progress.  
    * **Continuing Education Courses**: Clear organizational policy statements detailing student refund eligibility criteria and required timeframes for course completion.  
* **Violating Examples**:  
  * The syllabus references "all standard university policies apply" without providing direct links, explicit policy text, or directions on where students can access these policies.  
  * A course lacks any stated or linked policy regarding late work or academic integrity, leaving students unaware of penalties for late submissions or plagiarism.  
  * In a synchronous online course, the instructor enforces strict live webcam attendance and recording rules during real-time sessions, but no written policy explaining these synchronous rules is posted on the course site.

## **Standard 1.5**

* **ID**: GR-S1.5  
* **Category**: Course Overview and Introduction  
* **Summary**: Minimum technology requirements for the course are clearly stated, and information on how to obtain them is provided.  
* **Rubric Type**: Green  
* **Points**: 2pts  
* **Priority Weight**: Medium  
* **Scope of Implementation**: Course syllabus, course orientation / "Start Here" module, Technology Requirements page, and student resource/support pages.  
* **Passing Threshold**: Minimum 1 instance clearly listing minimum required technologies (hardware, software, peripherals) AND providing explicit instructions or direct links on how to obtain them on the course site at the start of the course.  
* **Annotation**: The word “technologies” covers a wide range, including hardware, software, peripherals (webcams, microphones, etc.), subscriptions, plug-ins, and mobile applications (apps). The technologies required to complete course activities are clearly listed. Course information specifies which course activities require computer use and which may be completed with different types of hardware (mobile devices vs. computers) and/or software. Learners are provided with detailed information on where and how they can obtain the technologies required for the course. The course software is obtainable via download and/or is institutionally provided and available on a variety of platforms (Windows, MacOS, Android, iOS, etc.). If software is available on only one platform, learners are notified about the specific limitations and required use.   
* **Rationales**: Clearly specifying minimum technology requirements and providing explicit instructions on how to acquire them ensures that learners are fully equipped to participate in course activities without encountering technical barriers or unexpected delays. Explaining platform compatibility, hardware limitations, required peripherals, and access options (including institutional software licenses and low-cost/no-cost resources) creates an equitable learning environment and eliminates technical friction during task completion.   
* **Note to Reviewers**: Look in the course syllabus, "Start Here" orientation module, or a dedicated Technology Requirements page for information that clearly states the minimum technology requirements and instructions on how to obtain them. Confirm that "technologies" encompasses required hardware, software, peripherals (speakers, microphones, webcams, AR/VR/XR accessories), subscriptions, plug-ins (e.g., PDF readers), and mobile apps. Verify that course information specifies which activities require a computer versus those compatible with mobile devices. Check that direct links or instructions are provided for downloading software, accessing institutional databases/library subscriptions, obtaining publisher access codes, and utilizing online proctoring services (including device limitations). Look for information regarding no-cost or low-cost options (e.g., institutional hardware loaner programs, campus computer labs, mobile hotspots).   
* **Hybrid Courses**: N/A  
* **Competency-Based Courses**: N/A  
* **Synchronous Courses**: N/A  
* **Aligned Elements**:  
  *  **Hardware, Software, & Peripheral Specifications**:  
    * A clearly stated list of minimum technology requirements, including computer operating systems (e.g., Windows, MacOS, iOS, Android) and hardware specifications.  
    * Explicit specifications for required hardware peripherals, such as webcams, microphones, headsets, speakers, or extended reality accessories (AR/VR/XR).  
    * Explicit clarification detailing which course activities require desktop/laptop computers versus those that can be completed on mobile devices or tablets.   
    * Guidance specifying which course activities require computer use versus those that may be completed using mobile devices or alternative hardware/software.  
    * Explicit notification if required software is restricted to a single operating system platform (e.g., Windows-only or MacOS-only) along with required usage expectations.   
  * **Downloadable Resources & Application Links**:  
    * Direct download links or detailed step-by-step instructions for acquiring required software, mobile applications, plug-ins (e.g., PDF readers, media players), and interactive multimedia tools.  
    * Step-by-step instructions for acquiring, redeeming, and accessing required publisher access codes or digital materials.  
    * Clear instructions and direct access links for library resources, online academic databases, and institutional subscription services.  
  * **Specialized Technical & Proctoring Tools**:  
    * Detailed descriptions of proctoring or academic integrity services utilized in the course, including technical device limitations and installation instructions.  
    * Guidance on how to access and set up interactive multimedia applications, web-conferencing tools, or external social media platforms used for coursework.  
  * **Acquisition Guidance & Affordable Technology Options**:  
    * Detailed information outlining exactly where and how learners can obtain all technologies required for the course (e.g., institutionally provided software downloads).  
    * Links and contact information directing learners to low-cost or no-cost technology alternatives, such as institutional hardware loaner programs, campus computer labs, or Wi-Fi hotspots.  
* **Violating Examples**:  
  * A course requires students to participate in live video discussions and submit recorded presentations, but fails to list a webcam or microphone under the course technology requirements or explain how students can acquire them.  
  * The syllabus states that "specialized statistical software is required for this course," but provides no download links, instructions on where to purchase or download the software, or information on whether institutional licenses exist.  
  * A course utilizes online proctoring software for major exams, but provides no guidance on device compatibility, system requirements, or setup instructions on the course site prior to the exam.  
  * An assignment requires specific software that only runs on Windows PCs, but the course site fails to notify Mac users of this limitation or provide alternative access options (such as campus computer labs or virtual desktop environments).

## **Standard 1.6**

* **ID**: GR-S1.6  
* **Category**: Course Overview and Introduction  
* **Summary**: Technical skills and digital information literacy skills expected of the learner are clearly stated.  
* **Rubric Type**: Green  
* **Points**: 1pt  
* **Priority Weight**: Low  
* **Scope of Implementation**: Course syllabus, course orientation / "Start Here" module, Technology Requirements page, and student support/resource pages.  
* **Passing Threshold**: Minimum 1 instance clearly stating expected general technical skills, course-specific computer skills, and digital information literacy skills (along with corresponding help/support resources) on the course site at the start of the course.  
* **Annotation**: General as well as course-specific technical skills learners must have to succeed in the course are specified. Digital information literacy refers to the ability to locate, evaluate, apply, create, and communicate knowledge using technology. Reviewers: Look in the course syllabus or other course documents for information that clearly states the technical and digital information literacy skills needed for the course.   
* **Rationales**: Explicitly identifying expected general computer skills, course-specific software proficiencies, and digital information literacy skills allows learners to assess their technological readiness before engaging with academic tasks. Pairing clear skill expectations with accessible technical support resources minimizes technology-related anxiety, eliminates friction during assignment completion, and ensures students can effectively navigate, evaluate, and communicate digital information.   
* **Note to Reviewers**: Examine the course syllabus, "Start Here" orientation module, or dedicated Technology Requirements page for clear statements regarding expected technical skills (both general computer skills and course-specific skills) and digital information literacy skills. Confirm that help resources and technical support links are provided alongside these expectations to assist learners in acquiring or troubleshooting necessary skills.   
* **Hybrid Courses**: N/A  
* **Competency-Based Courses**: N/A  
* **Synchronous Courses**: N/A  
* **Aligned Elements**:  
  * **General Technical & LMS Skills**:  
    * Clear statements of essential computer skills required to navigate and utilize the Learning Management System (LMS).  
    * Expectations for managing digital files, including creating, formatting, and submitting assignments in standard word-processing formats.  
    * Proficiency expectations for using email, including sending messages with attached documents.  
    * Basic software management skills, such as downloading, installing, and updating required applications or browser plug-ins.  
    * Ability to navigate digital devices and utilize standard mobile applications relevant to the course.  
  * **Course-Specific & Advanced Technical Skills**:  
    * Explicit listing of specialized computer skills needed to successfully complete course-specific assignments and activities.  
    * Skills required for organizing data, creating formulas, and running analyses using spreadsheet software.  
    * Competencies in building and delivering visual presentations using presentation and graphics software.  
    * Technical proficiency required to operate web conferencing tools, virtual meeting software, and interactive digital applications.  
  * **Digital Information Literacy Skills**:  
    * Clear expectations regarding the ability to locate, gather, and manage academic information using university libraries, online research databases, and computer networks.   
    * Skills in using academic search engines, online libraries, and specialized databases to locate and gather scholarly research.  
    * Mastery of search criteria, Boolean operators, keywords, and database filters to execute specific academic queries.  
    * Competencies in evaluating digital information for authority, credibility, currency, accuracy, and bias (including identifying misinformation and disinformation).  
    * Skills in using computer networks and cloud storage to locate, back up, and store academic files or research data.

## **Standard 2.1**

* **ID**: GR-S2.1  
* **Category**: Learning Outcomes  
* **Summary**: The course-level learning outcomes describe outcomes that are measurable.  
* **Rubric Type**: Green  
* **Points**: 3pts  
* **Priority Weight**: High  
* **Scope of Implementation**: Course syllabus, course orientation / "Start Here" module, Course Overview page, and course-wide curriculum planning documentation.  
* **Passing Threshold**: At least 85% of course-level learning outcomes present in the course must be measurable (or, if course outcomes are institutionally mandated and non-measurable, 100% of instructor-written module/unit-level outcomes must be measurable and aligned with the course outcomes).  
* **Annotation**: The concept of alignment is intended to convey the idea that critical course components work together to ensure that learners achieve the desired learning outcomes. Measurable course-level and module/unit-level learning outcomes form the basis of alignment in a course. Other elements of the course, including those addressed in Specific Review Standards 2.2, 3.1, 4.1, 5.1, and 6.1, contribute to the accomplishment of the learning outcomes. Measurable course-level learning outcomes precisely and clearly describe what learners will be able to do if they successfully complete the course. Course-level outcomes describe desired learner achievement using terms that are specific and observable enough to be measured by the instructor. Reviewers look for measurable learning outcomes that describe what learners will be able to do once they “understand” or “know” or “realize” a concept in the course. For example, a learning outcome that calls for the learner to “understand the nature of reasoning” could become a measurable learning outcome by replacing “understand” with the verb “explain”: “Explain the nature of reasoning.” If learners are expected to demonstrate “core competencies,” such as analytical skills or the ability to express themselves effectively in writing or in other forms of communication, the course includes a reference to these foundational, core outcomes in addition to outcomes that relate to course-specific learning achievement. In addition to measurable outcomes, a course may have outcomes that are not easily measured, such as increased awareness of, sensitivity to, or interest in certain issues or subjects, or ability to work as a team member on a group project. Such outcomes cannot be substituted for measurable outcomes when determining whether Specific Review Standard 2.1 is met. For the Specific Review Standard to be met, eighty-five percent (85%) of the course-level outcomes must be measurable.   
* **Rationales**: Measurable course-level learning outcomes form the primary foundation of course alignment, clearly defining observable expectations for student achievement upon course completion. By replacing passive mental verbs (e.g., "understand," "know," "learn") with active performance verbs, measurable outcomes provide learners with explicit goals and enable instructors to reliably assess skill acquisition across assessments, instructional materials, learning activities, and tools.   
* **Note to Reviewers**: Examine the course-level outcomes (found in the syllabus, Course Overview page, or LMS orientation module) to verify that at least 85% use specific, observable verbs describing what learners will be able to *do*. Avoid passive mental behaviors like "understand," "know," "learn," "realize," "be aware of," or "appreciate," ensuring they are translated into observable actions (e.g., "explain," "select," "analyze," "create," "apply"). Verify that institutionally required core competencies (e.g., writing-across-the-curriculum) are included alongside discipline outcomes. **Special Situations for Institutionally Mandated Outcomes:** Check the Course Worksheet. If course-level outcomes are institutionally mandated and non-measurable, leave them as they are and evaluate whether instructor-written module/unit-level outcomes are measurable and aligned (Standard 2.1 is met if module outcomes are measurable and aligned). Provide recommendations for institutional improvement in your feedback. **CRITICAL:** Standard 2.1 is NOT met if there are no stated course outcomes, if instructor-set course outcomes are non-measurable, or if non-measurable institutional course outcomes lack measurable/aligned module outcomes. If Standard 2.1 is NOT met, the review must be suspended and reported to the Team Chair immediately, as it is impossible to proceed with the course review.   
* **Hybrid Courses**: N/A  
* **Competency-Based Courses**: N/A  
* **Synchronous Courses**: N/A  
* **Aligned Elements**:  
  * **Measurable Verbs & Observable Performance Indicators**:  
    * Course-level outcomes formulated with specific, observable action verbs that describe what learners will be able to *do* upon completing the course (e.g., *Select, Develop, Demonstrate, Articulate, Apply, Create, Analyze, Explain*).  
    * Clear statements describing concrete student achievements upon course completion (e.g., "Select appropriate tax strategies," "Create original musical compositions using computer technology," "Apply microeconomic principles to explain why environmental problems occur").   
    * Explicit avoidance or replacement of unmeasurable, passive mental verbs (e.g., replacing *understand, know, learn, realize, appreciate, or be aware of* with measurable verbs like *explain, evaluate, or perform*).  
    * Outcome statements structured so that instructor evaluation can directly measure learner achievement.  
    * Compliance with the requirement that at least eighty-five percent (85%) of all course-level outcomes are measurable.  
  * **Core Competencies & Affective Learning Distinctions**:  
    * Direct inclusion of foundational or institutional core competencies where required (e.g., analytical skills, writing-across-the-curriculum metrics, or formal communication skills) alongside discipline-specific outcomes when institutionally required (e.g., writing-across-the-curriculum requirements).   
    * Clear distinction between core measurable outcomes and non-measurable affective goals (e.g., increased awareness, sensitivity, subject interest, or teamwork appreciation), ensuring affective goals do not replace required measurable outcomes.  
  * **Structural Foundation for Course Alignment**:  
    * Outcome formulations that provide an explicit baseline for aligning module-level outcomes (2.2), assessments (3.1), instructional materials (4.1), learning activities (5.1), and course tools (6.1).  
  * **Alignment Foundation & Location Anchors**:  
    * Course-level outcomes prominently stated in the course syllabus, Course Overview page, or introductory orientation materials.  
    * Outcomes formatted to establish the structural foundation for alignment with module-level outcomes, assessments, instructional materials, learning activities, and course tools.  
  * **Institutional Mandates & Special Situations**:  
    * **Institutionally Mandated Outcomes**: In cases where course-level outcomes are mandated by the institution/department and cannot be modified by the instructor, the standard is met if:  
      * The institutionally mandated course-level outcomes are already measurable, OR  
      * The non-measurable mandated course-level outcomes are supported by instructor-written module/unit-level outcomes that are fully measurable and aligned.  
    * Written recommendations provided for institutional review to reframe non-measurable mandated course outcomes into measurable action terms.  
* **Violating Examples**:  
  * Course-level outcomes rely entirely on unmeasurable, passive mental verbs such as "Understand the nature of reasoning," "Demonstrate an appreciation of contemporary art," or "Know basic statistical vocabulary."  
  * The course syllabus lists zero course-level learning outcomes, leaving the overall learning goals of the course completely unstated.  
  * Instructor-written course outcomes consist of vague, unobservable objectives (e.g., "Be aware of grammar conventions" or "Realize the significance of genetic research") without specifying how performance will be demonstrated or evaluated.  
  * Institutionally mandated course-level outcomes are non-measurable (e.g., "Learn basic media production software"), and the instructor has failed to write measurable module-level learning outcomes or align them with the overarching course bjectives.

## **Standard 2.2**

* **ID**: GR-S2.2  
* **Category**: Learning Outcomes  
* **Summary**: The module/unit-level learning outcomes are measurable and consistent with the course-level learning outcomes.  
* **Rubric Type**: Green  
* **Points**: 3pts  
* **Priority Weight**: High  
* **Scope of Implementation**: Course-wide, across every weekly or thematic lesson module, unit overview page, assignment prompt, and learning module throughout the course site.  
* **Passing Threshold**: 100% of all lesson modules/units present in the course must contain stated module/unit-level learning outcomes that are both measurable and aligned with (and more specific than) the course-level learning outcomes.  
* **Annotation**: The concept of alignment is intended to convey the idea that critical course components work together to ensure that learners achieve the desired learning outcomes. Measurable module/unit-level learning outcomes form the basis of alignment in a course because they are consistent with the course-level outcomes (2.1). Outcomes align with learner assessment (3.1), instructional materials (4.1), learning activities (5.1), and tools used in the course (6.1) contribute to the accomplishment of the learning outcomes. Learning outcomes at the module/unit level align with and are more specific than course-level learning outcomes. The module/unit-level learning outcomes describe learner proficiency in specific, observable terms and in smaller, discrete pieces. The outcomes describe the specific competencies, skills, and knowledge learners are able to demonstrate at regular intervals throughout the course. The module/unit-level outcomes are aligned with the course-level outcomes. If alignment is not evident, consult with the Subject Matter Expert on the team to determine if alignment is present.   
* **Rationales**: Measurable module-level learning outcomes form the essential structural foundation of course alignment, breaking down broad course-level outcomes into discrete, observable, and assessable milestones. By describing student performance in specific terms at regular intervals throughout the course, measurable module outcomes allow learners to track their progress incrementally and ensure that assessments, instructional materials, learning activities, and course tools work cohesively toward target mastery.   
* **Note to Reviewers**: Verify that every module/unit contains stated outcomes, that all module/unit-level outcomes are measurable (using observable, action-oriented verbs), and that they align with and are more specific than the course-level outcomes. Standard 2.2 is met if instructor-written module outcomes are measurable and aligned with course outcomes (even if institutionally mandated course outcomes are not measurable). Standard 2.2 is NOT met if module outcomes are missing, unmeasurable, or misaligned with course outcomes. **CRITICAL:** If Standard 2.2 is not met, it is impossible to complete the course review; the review is suspended, and the Team Chair must consult the Course Representative to determine if the issue can be quickly resolved so the review can continue. Consult with the team Subject Matter Expert (SME) if alignment is not immediately evident.   
* **Hybrid Courses**: N/A  
* **Competency-Based Courses**: N/A  
* **Synchronous Courses**: N/A  
* **Aligned Elements**:  
  * **Measurability & Granularity Indicators**:  
    * Module/unit-level learning outcomes written with observable, measurable action verbs (e.g., *Identify, Analyze, Discuss, Evaluate, Articulate*).  
    * Outcomes framed in discrete, smaller pieces that describe specific learner proficiency and competencies demonstrated at regular intervals (e.g., weekly or per module/unit).  
    * Complete avoidance of unmeasurable or passive verbs at the module level.  
    * Module outcomes that are narrower and more specific than overarching course-level outcomes, describing learner proficiency in smaller, discrete pieces.   
  * **Course-to-Module Alignment & Structural Hierarchy**:  
    * Clear conceptual alignment where module-level outcomes serve as actionable stepping stones that directly support and aggregate into course-level outcomes (e.g., breaking down a broad course outcome on analyzing historical sentiment into discrete module outcomes focusing on identifying primary sources, evaluating bias, analyzing literature, and engaging in peer discussions).   
    * Direct vertical alignment ensuring module-level outcomes are consistent with and directly support overarching course-level learning outcomes.  
    * Module/unit outcomes designed to be strictly more specific than course-level outcomes, detailing the specific knowledge, skills, and practical tasks required to achieve broader course goals.  
    * Modular outcomes serving as the core alignment anchor for weekly assessments (3.1), instructional materials (4.1), learning activities (5.1), and course technology tools (6.1).  
  * **Placement, Visibility, & Presentation Anchors**:  
    * Every individual module or unit explicitly includes its own dedicated set of module/unit-level learning outcomes.  
    * Outcomes prominently displayed in corresponding module overview pages, weekly landing pages, or unit task lists where learners access them from within the course site.  
  * **Special Situations & Institutional Mandate Criteria**:  
    * **Mandated Course Outcomes**: In courses where institutional or departmental mandates force the use of unmeasurable course-level outcomes, instructor-written module/unit-level outcomes must be fully measurable and clearly aligned with the intended scope of those course outcomes to meet the standard.  
* **Violating Examples**:  
  * A course provides overarching course-level outcomes on the syllabus, but several weekly modules contain no stated module/unit-level learning outcomes at all.  
  * Module outcomes are written using vague, unmeasurable phrasing (e.g., "Students will understand World War II," "Learners will appreciate secondary sources," or "Gain familiarity with historical analysis") that cannot be directly observed or evaluated.  
  * A module outcome focuses on a topic completely unrelated to the rest of the course (e.g., basic statistical programming) that shares no conceptual connection or alignment with the stated course-level outcomes.  
  * Module-level outcomes are written at a broader, more abstract level than the course-level outcomes, failing to break down competencies into smaller, discrete, and measurable learning components.

## **Standard 2.3**

* **Annotation**: The course and module/unit-level learning outcomes are clearly stated. The use of educational or discipline jargon, unexplained terminology, and unnecessarily complex language is avoided. The course and module/unit-level learning outcomes are prominently located in the course site. The course-level outcomes may be articulated in the course introduction or syllabus. Module/Unit-level learning outcomes are prominently stated in the corresponding module or unit so they are available to the learner from within the course site.

Prominently positioning course-level and module-level outcomes in predictable locations (such as Course Overview and Module Overview template pages) allows learners to easily reference expectations, align their study efforts, and take ownership of their learning journey.   
* **Note to Reviewers**: Confirm that all three parts of this Specific Review Standard are met: (1) outcomes are clearly stated without unexplained jargon or overly complex language; (2) outcomes are learner-centered (written from the learner's perspective, describing what the learner will be able to do); and (3) outcomes are prominently located in the course site (course-level outcomes in the course introduction, syllabus, or Course Overview page; module-level outcomes prominently stated on corresponding Module Overview pages). If only one or two parts are met, mark this standard as "Not Met."   
* **Hybrid Courses**: In addition to being provided in the face-to-face classroom, learning outcomes are clearly and prominently stated on the online course site.   
* **Competency-Based Courses**: N/A  
* **Synchronous Courses**: In addition to being provided in the virtual classroom, learning outcomes are clearly and prominently stated on the online course site.   
* **Aligned Elements**:  
  * **Clarity & Accessible Language Elements**:  
    * Outcomes stated clearly using direct, accessible language that is easy for learners to understand.

    * Outcome statements written without unnecessarily complex sentence structures or convoluted phrasing.

Reviewers: Examine the course and module or unit learning outcomes for the course as a whole to ensure they describe knowledge and skills that correspond to the course level. Aligning outcome verbs and achievement criteria with established learning taxonomies (such as Bloom's Taxonomy) ensures that lower-division, upper-division, or graduate-level courses challenge students appropriately while scaffolding skill acquisition effectively from baseline comprehension to advanced application.   
* **Note to Reviewers**: Examine the course-level and module/unit-level learning outcomes for the course as a whole to ensure they describe knowledge, skills, and performance standards that correspond to the course level. Use established learning taxonomies (e.g., Bloom's Taxonomy) as evaluative tools. Note that course levels do not exclusively use a single taxonomy level; lower-division courses may scaffold from lower to higher cognitive levels, while graduate courses may include foundational knowledge checks. Reviewers should apply professional judgment, professional experience, and consult with the review team's Subject Matter Expert (SME) or the course instructor when evaluating discipline-specific performance standards.

* **Competency-Based Courses**: N/A  
* **Synchronous Courses**: N/A  
* **Aligned Elements**:  
  * **Level-Appropriate Outcome Statements**: :  
    * Outcome statements describing precise knowledge and skill expectations that reflect appropriate academic depth (e.g., *"Write a well-organized research paper, with appropriate sources and citations"* rather than vague phrasing like *"Write a paper"*).  
    * Technical or quantitative outcomes specifying the required degree of mastery (e.g., *"Apply the appropriate theorem to correctly solve mathematical equations"* rather than unspecific statements like *"Apply theorems"*).  
  * **Course Division & Progressive Complexity**:  
    * Introductory or lower-division course designs that deliberately scaffold learning objectives from foundational cognitive verbs (e.g., "Distinguish between a persuasive speech and an informative speech") to higher-level performance objectives (e.g., "Deliver a persuasive speech").

# **General Standard 3**

* **Category**: Assessments  
* **General Standard Summary**: Assessments are integral to the learning process and are designed to evaluate learner progress in achieving the stated learning outcomes.  
* **General Standard Overview**: Assessment is implemented in a manner that corresponds to the course learning outcomes and not only allows the instructor a broad perspective on the learners’ achievement, but also allows learners to track their learning progress throughout the course.

## **Standard 3.1**

* **ID**: GR-S3.1  
* **Category**: Assessments  
* **Summary**: The assessments measure the achievement of the stated learning outcomes.  
* **Rubric Type**: Green  
* **Points**: 3pts  
* **Priority Weight**: High  
* **Scope of Implementation**: Course-wide, integrated across lesson modules, quizzes, exams, discussion prompts, performance-based assessments, capstone projects, lab reports, and authentic assignments.  
* **Passing Threshold**: 85% of total examined course assessments present in the course must directly measure learner achievement of stated course-level or module/unit-level learning outcomes (matching both the cognitive level and performance domain required by the objectives).  
* **Annotation**: Course assessments (ways of confirming learner progress and achievement) are consistent with the course and module/unit-level learning outcomes (2.1 and 2.2) by measuring learning progress or achievement. Instructional materials (4.1), learning activities (5.1), and course tools (6.1) support the learning outcomes and enable learners to be successful on the assessments. Assessments (instruments)—instruments used to evaluate learning progress (e.g., formative assessments) and achievement (e.g., summative assessments)—measure what students have learned; specifically, they measure learner achievement of learning outcomes. The Specific Review Standard is met if the assessments measure learner achievement of the outcomes.   
* **Rationales**: Direct alignment between course assessments and learning outcomes ensures that tests, projects, and assignments accurately measure whether students have mastered the intended knowledge and skills. When formative and summative instruments match the specific cognitive level and domain specified in module- and course-level objectives, they provide valid, reliable measures of student achievement, prevent misaligned grading, and ensure that instructional materials, activities, and tools effectively prepare learners for success.   
* **Note to Reviewers**: Examine both course-level and module-level outcomes alongside all course assessments (including projects, quiz/exam questions, discussion prompts, performance-based assessments, authentic tasks, etc.) to ensure that the assessments directly measure the specified learning outcomes. Verify that the cognitive level and performance task of the assessment match the action verb of the objective (e.g., if the outcome calls for "delivering" or "preparing," the assessment must require actual delivery or creation rather than simple written summary or analysis). Special Situations: If course-level learning outcomes are institutionally mandated and misaligned with specific assignments (check the Course Worksheet), evaluate alignment strictly against the module/unit-level learning outcomes. Note that institutional writing-across-the-curriculum or program outcomes may require suggesting outcome updates if not explicitly listed in the course.   
* **Hybrid Courses**: A copy of any assessment delivered in real time, such as an in-class exam, live presentation, or verbal quiz, is posted on the course site for the purpose of the course review.   
* **Competency-Based Courses**: Learners have flexibility in preparing for assessment of competencies, as they may have acquired competencies in a work environment, through life experience, or independent study. Focus on whether the assessment instruments credibly establish that the learner has demonstrated achievement of the competency.   
* **Synchronous Courses**: A copy of any assessment delivered in real time, such as a live web-based exam, synchronous oral quiz, or real-time presentation, is posted on the course site for the purpose of the course review.   
* **Aligned Elements**:  
  * **Direct Outcome Measurement & Alignment Fidelity**:  
    * Strict alignment between assessment tasks and the action verbs in stated learning outcomes (e.g., delivering a speech for an oral delivery outcome, preparing an operating budget for a budget creation outcome, or writing a lab report for an experimental procedure outcome).  
    * Avoidance of assessment mode mismatches (e.g., ensuring students are not assessed via multiple-choice recall when the outcome requires higher-order analysis, or writing a paper when the outcome requires live performance).  
    * Inclusion of assessments for required institutional core competencies, such as writing-across-the-curriculum requirements.  
  * **Formative & Summative Measurement Systems:**  
    * Formative assessments (e.g., practice quizzes, preliminary drafts, peer reviews) and summative assessments (e.g., final exams, capstone papers, portfolios) that systematically measure progress toward outcome mastery.  
  * **Integration with Supporting Components**:   
    * Assessments designed to directly evaluate concepts presented in instructional materials (4.1), practiced in learning activities (5.1), and facilitated by course tools (6.1).   
    * Balance of formative evaluations (to track ongoing learning progress) and summative evaluations (to measure final outcome achievement).  
  * **Institutional Mandates & Special Situations**:  
    * **Mandated Course Outcomes**: In courses where course-level learning outcomes are institutionally mandated and misaligned with specific assignments, assessment alignment is evaluated directly against instructor-written module/unit-level learning outcomes.  
  * **Modality-Specific Assessment Criteria**:  
    * **Hybrid and Synchronous Courses**: Copies, prompts, or detailed descriptions of all real-time assessments (e.g., in-class exams, verbal quizzes, synchronous live presentations) posted on the asynchronous LMS course site for review and student reference.  
    * **Competency-Based Courses**: Flexible assessment options that allow learners to credibly establish and demonstrate competency mastery acquired through prior work environment experience, life experience, or independent study.  
    * **Continuing Education Courses**: Assessments centered around authentic professional products, practical occupational artifacts, or earning professional/occupational certifications.  
* **Violating Examples**:  
  * A module learning outcome requires students to "deliver a persuasive speech," but the assessment requires students to write an essay or speech outline without ever recording or delivering the speech.  
  * An advanced nursing outcome specifies that learners will "describe epidemiological causes of major health problems in vulnerable populations," but the corresponding assessment is a low-level multiple-choice quiz requiring learners merely to identify or list vulnerable populations.  
  * A finance course outcome requires students to "prepare an operating budget within a comprehensive budget," but the assessment asks students to interpret or critique a pre-made sample budget rather than creating one.  
  * In a hybrid or synchronous course, exams or oral presentations are conducted in-person or live during web sessions, but zero documentation, prompts, or copies of the assessment instruments are posted on the LMS course site for review.

## **Standard 3.2**

* **ID**: GR-S3.2  
* **Category**: Assessments  
* **Summary**: The course grading policy is stated clearly, available at the beginning of the course, and consistent throughout the course site.  
* **Rubric Type**: Green  
* **Points**: 3pts  
* **Priority Weight**: High  
* **Scope of Implementation**: Course syllabus, course orientation / "Start Here" module, Course Overview page, grading policy page, LMS gradebook configuration, and individual assignment/quiz descriptions across the course site.  
* **Passing Threshold**: 100% compliance across all three core conditions: (1) clearly stated grading policy (including explicit late work rules); (2) availability at the beginning of the course; and (3) complete structural consistency between the published policy, gradebook setup, and individual assessment point/weight listings throughout the course site.  
* **Annotation**: A clear, written statement at the beginning of the course fully explains to the learner how the course grades are calculated. Depending on the grading system used, points, percentages, and weights for each component of the course grade are clearly stated, and the relationship(s) between points, percentages, weights, and letter grades are explained. If an alternative grading approach is used, the approach is clearly explained, including how work will be evaluated and by whom. Late work is addressed in the grading policy. If grades are reduced because of late submission, the instructor’s policy on late submission clearly states the amount of the reduction. If there is no penalty for late submissions, or late work is considered on an individual basis, this policy is clearly explained.   
* **Rationales**: Providing a transparent, comprehensive, and consistent grading policy at the very start of the course enables learners to understand how their academic performance is evaluated and how final grades are calculated. Explicitly detailing point values, category weightings, letter grade scales, and late work policies reduces student anxiety, fosters trust, supports self-directed time management, and ensures equitable evaluation across all course components.   
* **Note to Reviewers**: Review the clarity of the explanation and presentation to the learner, not the simplicity or complexity of the grading system itself. Confirm that all three conditions are met for this standard to be marked "Met": (1) the grading policy (traditional or alternative) is clearly explained and addresses late submissions; (2) the policy is available when the course begins (in the syllabus, "Start Here" folder, or Course Overview page); and (3) the grading policy is completely consistent for assignments, quizzes, and projects throughout the course site (e.g., if the syllabus indicates a points-based system, references in individual assessments reflect points rather than mismatched percentages or letter grades). If any of the three conditions are missing, mark this standard as "Not Met." Continuing Education Courses: If grades are not used (e.g., personal enrichment), check the Course Worksheet; if grades are omitted by design, mark this standard as "Met."   
* **Hybrid Courses**: If information on course grading is shared live during the first class session, a copy of the information is posted on the course site for learners to reference asynchronously.   
* **Competency-Based Courses**: The grading pattern may be different from that used in traditionally graded courses, but the grading policy is equally essential to describe. Competency-based grades may rely exclusively on demonstration of achievement of stated competencies (which may occur within the course or via an external process) and may take the form of a transcript listing competencies attained.   
* **Synchronous Courses**: If information on course grading is shared live during the first class session, a copy of the information is posted on the course site for learners to reference asynchronously.   
* **Aligned Elements**:  
  * **Transparent Grade Calculation & Breakdown Structure**:   
    * Clear written explanation of how the final course grade is calculated, detailing the relationship between accumulated points, percentages, category weights, and final letter grades.   
    * Complete list of all course assignments, quizzes, and projects showing exact point values (and category weights, if applicable) along with an explanation of how weighted totals are computed.  
    * An explanation clarifying the relationship between accumulated points and percentages if both systems are referenced in the course.  
    * A complete conversion table mapping specific point totals or percentages to equivalent final letter grades.  
    * An explanation detailing the calculation method used when course assignments are weighted.  
  * **Late Work & Alternative Grading Policy Rules**:  
    * An explicit policy statement governing late submissions, including exact point reduction amounts or percentage deductions.  
    * Clear statements explaining if there is no penalty for late submissions, or specifying that late work is considered on an individual, case-by-case basis upon contacting the instructor.  
  * **Alternative Grading Approach Frameworks**:  
    * Detailed explanations of non-traditional or alternative grading models (e.g., ungrading, labor-based contract grading, peer evaluation, pass/fail, or complete/incomplete models), explicitly explaining how work is evaluated and by whom.  
  * **Consistency, Availability, & Location Anchors**:  
    * Complete grading policy posted prominently prior to or at the start of the course within the syllabus, "Start Here" / orientation folder, or on a dedicated course policy page.  
    * Strict consistency in grading terminology and point values across the syllabus, assignment prompts, rubrics, and LMS Gradebook settings (e.g., avoiding conflicting point listings between the syllabus and LMS).  
  * **Modality-Specific Grading Criteria**:  
    * **Hybrid and Synchronous Courses**: A copy of course grading policy information posted in the online course site for student reference if the policy is presented live during the first class session.  
    * **Competency-Based Courses**: Explicit policies explaining how grades or transcript records rely on demonstrating achievement of defined competencies.  
    * **Continuing Education Courses**: Explicit policy statements outlining alternative grading systems (e.g., "Pass/Fail" or "Complete/Incomplete"), or confirmation on the worksheet if grades are not used.  
* **Violating Examples**:  
  * The syllabus states that weekly discussions are worth 10 points each, but the Canvas discussion assignment prompts and LMS gradebook are set to 20 points each, creating structural inconsistency across the course site.  
  * The syllabus states that final grades are calculated using weighted categories (e.g., Exams 40%, Quizzes 30%, Assignments 30%), but fails to provide a list of assignments or explain how earned points translate into weighted percentages and letter grades.  
  * A course assigns multiple high-stakes papers and quizzes but omits a late work policy entirely, leaving learners uninformed about penalties or procedures for late submissions.  
  * An instructor explains the grading policy and late submission rules verbally during a live synchronous session or hybrid first class meeting, but never posts a written copy on the asynchronous course site.  
  * The syllabus displays a point-based grading scale, but individual assignment instructions evaluate student submissions using an unexplained letter grade scale without providing numerical point values or rubric conversions.

* **Annotation**: Assessments provide multiple ways for learners to demonstrate progress and achievement and are designed to accommodate diverse learners. Courses might use a combination of assessment types, including exams, essays, discussions, reflective journals, group projects, portfolios, written papers, presentations, multimedia projects, and interviews. Additionally, some course designs might provide assessment pathways that enable learners to choose from several assessment options. For example, a learner who is adept at storytelling might be assessed on developing a video about the concepts learned in the course. The assessments are sequenced in a way that enables learners to build on previous knowledge and skills gained in this course and prerequisite courses. Assessments are paced to give learners adequate time to complete work in a thoughtful manner and demonstrate learning achievement. To determine whether the assessments are suited to the level of the course, refer to Specific Review Standards 2.5 and 3.1. “Explain,” for example, could be used in a learning objective for a low-level or a high-level assessment. Look at the course as a whole to determine if the Specific Review Standard is met, since individual modules may not include sequenced or multiple types of assessments. While assessments may reflect varying levels of cognitive engagement, it is important for learners to have the opportunity to apply what they are learning. If any of the three parts of this Specific Review Standard are not present (variety of types, sequenced, suited-to-level) then this Standard is marked as “Not Met.”   
* **Rationales**: Incorporating diverse assessment types accommodates varied learner abilities and communication strengths, ensuring equitable opportunities to demonstrate mastery. Structuring assessments in a deliberate, scaffolded sequence enables learners to progressively build upon foundational knowledge and prerequisite skills without being overwhelmed. Furthermore, aligning assessment complexity with the appropriate academic level ensures rigorous application of concepts, thoughtful pacing, and valid measurement of higher- or lower-order learning outcomes.   
* **Note to Reviewers**: Look at the course as a whole to determine if the standard is met, since individual modules may not include sequenced or multiple types of assessments. (Refer to Standard 1.2 for structure/sequencing, Standard 2.1 for application, and Standard 2.5 for course level.) Consult with the Subject Matter Expert (SME) on the team when considering discipline, course type, and academic level. Confirm that ALL THREE required elements are present: (1) variety of assessment types, (2) logical sequencing/pacing, and (3) suitability for the course level. If ANY of these three elements is missing, mark the standard as "Not Met." In graduate courses where grades depend heavily on a final major capstone project, verify that intermediate progress benchmarks (e.g., project plan, bibliography, précis, preliminary drafts) with instructor/peer feedback are provided during the term. For short continuing education courses (e.g., 1 module), a single assessment with fewer feedback loops may suffice.   
* **Hybrid Courses**: Copies of or information about assessments completed in real time during live or in-person meetings are included in the course site for review purposes.   
* **Competency-Based Courses**: Assessment of competencies may not follow the pattern of assessment in traditional courses. Focus on whether the assessment instruments credibly establish that the learner has demonstrated achievement of the competency.  
* **Synchronous Courses**: Copies of or information about assessments completed in real time during live sessions are included in the course site for review purposes.  
* **Aligned Elements**:  
  * **Assessment Variety & Flexible Pathways**:  
    * Inclusion of a balanced combination of diverse assessment formats (e.g., exams, essays, discussions, reflective journals, group projects, portfolios, written papers, presentations, multimedia projects, and interviews).  
    * Provision of assessment choice pathways that enable learners to choose from several options to demonstrate learning achievement (e.g., developing a video presentation versus writing a term paper).  
    * Assessment designs structured to accommodate diverse learners and varied learning strengths.  
  * **Sequencing, Pacing, & Scaffolding Elements**:  
    * Assessment progression that systematically moves learners from foundational knowledge (e.g., defining terminology or taking baseline quizzes) to intermediate analysis (e.g., short papers, scenario discussions) and capstone application (e.g., comprehensive term papers, community solutions projects).  
    * Purposeful pacing that provides learners adequate time between assignments to reflect, complete work thoughtfully, and demonstrate learning achievement.  
    * Scaffolding and benchmark milestones for major term projects or papers (e.g., requiring preliminary submissions such as project plans, outlines, bibliographies, précis, or initial drafts for feedback prior to the final submission).  
  * **Course Level & Cognitive Suitability**:  
    * Assessment tasks tailored appropriately to the discipline, type, and academic level of the course.  
    * Assessment progression aligned with course depth (e.g., progressing from basic terminology quizzes to scenario-based discussions and analytical term papers in undergraduate courses, or from case study evaluations to original community solution proposals in graduate courses).  
    * Opportunities for learners to apply what they are learning across varying levels of cognitive engagement rather than relying exclusively on lower-level recall.  
  * **Modality-Specific Assessment Criteria**:  
    * **Hybrid and Synchronous Courses**: Prompts, copies, or detailed structural information regarding all real-time assessments (such as live presentations or in-class exams) posted in the online course site.  
  * **Major Project Benchmark Structures**:  
    * Integration of progress benchmarks for major single-assignment courses, such as project plans, annotated bibliographies, research précis, or draft submissions with peer/instructor feedback loops.  
* **Violating Examples**:  
  * An advanced, graduate-level environmental science course where the final grade relies exclusively on multiple-choice recall quizzes and short definition essays, providing no higher-level analysis, application, or synthesis assessments.  
  * A course where all assessments are unpaced and heavily backloaded at the very end of the semester (e.g., zero assessments during weeks 1–12, followed by an essay, term paper, and final exam in weeks 13, 14, and 15).  
  * An information literacy course where the very first assessment requires students to conduct complex library research before research methods and database skills have been covered in the course sequencing.  
  * An environmental design course that evaluates student learning solely through automated multiple-choice tests, providing no authentic design projects, portfolios, or applied performance assessments.

* **Competency-Based Courses**: N/A  
* **Synchronous Courses**: N/A

  * **Formative Feedback & Progress Tracking**:  
    * Incorporation of smaller, lower-stakes formative assessments timed throughout the course to provide frequent, substantive, and timely feedback.

  * Quizzes or auto-graded tests that only return a final numerical score without identifying missed questions, explaining correct choices, or providing guidance to help learners track progress.  
  * A course structure where a preliminary project draft is due only 2–3 days before the final submission, leaving insufficient time for learners to receive or apply instructor/peer feedback.  
  * A course where draft submissions receive completion credit, but zero qualitative feedback or suggestions for improvement are provided by the instructor or peers.

## **Standard 3.6**

* **Summary**: The assessments provide guidance to the learner about how to uphold academic integrity.  
* **Rubric Type**: Green  
* **Points**: 1pt  
* **Priority Weight**: Low

Learners, particularly in online courses, benefit from specific and contextualized information about how to comply with institutional academic integrity policies and standards (see Specific Review Standard 1.4) as they complete assessments in the course. This guidance enables learners to apply abstract principles of academic integrity to real situations within the course (and discipline). The Specific Review Standard is met if the course assessments incorporate or reflect how the institution’s academic integrity policies and standards are relevant to those assessments. Information about academic integrity may be located in the instructions for individual assessments, or general descriptions and applications of academic integrity may be described by type elsewhere in the course (e.g., a policy restricting peer collaboration on all course exams may be included in the syllabus). .  
* **Rationales**: Providing specific, contextualized guidance on academic integrity within course assessments enables learners to translate abstract institutional policies into actionable practices. Clear instructions regarding source attribution, permitted resources, collaboration boundaries, and generative AI rules build trust, foster fairness, prevent unintentional academic dishonesty, and equip students to uphold professional disciplinary standards.   
* **Note to Reviewers**: Consult the Subject Matter Expert (SME) on the team to determine if the guidance provided is effective for the course and its specific assessment types, as each discipline has distinct contextual needs. Look for information on appropriate source usage, proper citation/paraphrasing, permitted versus unpermitted exam aids, explicit rules on peer collaboration and sharing, and policies regarding whether and how Generative AI tools may be used and documented. Guidance may appear within individual task descriptions or in a centralized "Getting Started" policy breakdown.

* **Competency-Based Courses**: N/A  
* **Synchronous Courses**: N/A  
* **Aligned Elements**:  
  * **Source Attribution & Citation Guidance**:  
    * Specific instructions embedded within individual assessment prompts explaining the appropriate use of sources and ethical attribution.  
    * Resources and guidance detailing proper paraphrasing techniques to ensure ideas are ethically credited to original authors.  
    * Discipline-specific citation guidelines (e.g., APA, MLA, Chicago) and references to institutional writing support for academic papers and research projects.  
  * **Exam & Assessment Resource Rules**:  
    * Clear syllabus or prompt statements explicitly listing permitted resources for exams and quizzes (e.g., specific calculator models, a single double-sided sheet of handwritten notes).

# **General Standard 4**

* **Category**: Instructional Materials  
* **General Standard Summary**: A variety of contextualized instructional materials enables learners to achieve the stated learning outcomes.  
* **General Standard Overview**: The focus of this Standard is on supporting the course outcomes through instructional materials that prepare learners to effectively interact with course topics and ideas, rather than on qualitative judgments about the instructional materials.

## **Standard 4.2**

* **ID**: GR-S4.2  
* **Category**: Instructional Materials  
* **Summary**: The relationship between the use of instructional materials in the course and completion of learning activities and assessments is clearly explained.  
* **Rubric Type**: Green  
* **Points**: 3pts  
* **Priority Weight**: High  
* **Scope of Implementation**: Course-wide, integrated across lesson modules, module overviews, task lists, reading lists, assignment prompts, assessment instructions, external link descriptions, and publisher resource guidelines.  
* **Passing Threshold**: 85% of total examined instructional materials present in the course are accompanied by clear explanations detailing how they prepare learners for specific learning activities and assessments, with optional/required items explicitly distinguished (or clear introductory guidance provided in competency-based courses).  
* **Annotation**: Learners are provided with an explanation of how the instructional materials prepare them to complete learning activities and assessments. If optional instructional materials are provided and their use is recommended in the course, the materials are labeled as optional. The relevance of required and optional instructional materials is explained. Consider whether the relationship between the instructional materials and learning activities and assessments is reflected in both the order in which the materials are presented and in the explanations of the materials. An explanation describes the relationship between the learning material and its application to an activity and/or assessment. The explanations may be found in a module overview or task list, where the materials are placed or linked in the course, or in activity and/or assessment descriptions or instructions. In courses in which learners are expected to find their own learning materials, such as in learner-led discussions or research projects, guidelines are provided that assist the learner in identifying relevant materials and in distinguishing between required and optional materials and between scholarly and non-scholarly sources for academic works.   
* **Rationales**: Clearly explaining how instructional materials prepare learners for learning activities and assessments establishes explicit cognitive relevance, helping students understand *why* they are engaging with specific readings, videos, or tools. Logical sequencing and transparent guidance on required versus optional resources improve time management, boost self-directed learning, and guide students in effectively applying content knowledge directly to course tasks and evaluations.   
* **Note to Reviewers**: Confirm that instructional materials are provided in such a way as to be useful to the learner in completing learning activities and assessments. Check whether explanations describe the relationship between learning materials and their direct application to activities/assessments. Look for these explanations in module overviews, task lists, resource links, assignment prompts, or embedded directly within the instructional/learning sequence. Verify that optional materials are clearly labeled and that their relevance is explained. In courses where learners find their own materials, ensure guidelines exist for distinguishing required vs. optional and scholarly vs. non-scholarly sources.

    * Explicit guidelines for learner-led research projects assisting students in identifying relevant resources and distinguishing between required, optional, scholarly, and non-scholarly sources.  
  * **Assessment & Assignment Task Alignment**:

    * Quiz and exam overviews indicating which specific textbook chapters or module resources to study for preparation.  
  * **Placement, Sequence, & Task Integration**:  
    * Logical sequencing where instructional materials are presented prior to the specific activities and assessments that rely on them.  
    * Schedule of assigned readings accompanied by explanations of how those texts will be incorporated into online discussion forum posts.  
    * Quiz overviews and essay prompts explicitly identifying which chapters, module readings, or videos to review or cite.  
  * **Self-Directed Research & Material Selection Guidelines:**  
    * Guidelines assisting learners in projects or learner-led discussions where they find their own materials, helping them distinguish between scholarly and non-scholarly sources and required versus optional readings.  
  * **Modality-Specific Guidance**:  
    * **Hybrid and Synchronous Courses**: Explicit instructions detailing which materials prepare students for in-person class meetings or live synchronous sessions versus those designated for the asynchronous online portion of the course.  
    * **Competency-Based Courses**: Introductory orientation notes and labels clearly specifying whether materials and activities are required, supplemental, or optional for demonstrating competencies.

## **Standard 4.3**

* **ID**: GR-S4.3  
* **Category**: Instructional Materials  
* **Summary**: The course models the academic integrity expected of learners by providing both source references and permissions for use of instructional materials.  
* **Rubric Type**: Green  
* **Points**: 2pts  
* **Priority Weight**: Medium  
* **Scope of Implementation**: Course-wide, integrated across lesson modules, course syllabus, lecture slides, media resources, reading lists, images/graphics, and instructional content pages.  
* **Passing Threshold**: 85% of total examined instructional materials (including instructor-created materials, publisher content, images, videos, journal articles, slides, and OER) present in the course must include properly formatted source references and clear permissions or licensing designations (or a centralized master attribution section in the syllabus or course resource module).  
* **Annotation**: Instructional materials include source references, and permission has been obtained to utilize the materials in the course. Sources for materials used in the course are clearly identified with references. This expectation applies to instructor-created materials, journal articles, publisher materials, textbooks, images, graphic materials, tables, videos, audio recordings, websites, slides, and all forms of multimedia. The format of references in instructional materials follows the style prescribed in a recognized guide, such as APA, MLA, or Chicago. At minimum, a reference includes the author or owner name; date of publication; resource title, if supplied; URL or source, such as a publisher; and license type, if openly licensed. When an extensive body of material comes from a single source (e.g., instructional materials from a publisher), a single reference suffices. Reviewers might look for reference information in a list of materials, in the course syllabus, or in another course document. For example, under “Instructional Materials” in the syllabus, “All PowerPoint Files” may be included and then listed with an appropriately formatted reference, rather than individually citing each PowerPoint slide set in each module. A course might include a single document that lists the sources of all materials. If permission to copy or download and post digital materials has been received, the permission to use those materials is posted in the course alongside or beneath the material or in a single location such as a “Permissions” section in the syllabus. In such a case, learners are able to see that the use of the intellectual property in this manner was permitted by the property owner (author, publisher, etc.).   
* **Rationales**: Modeling academic integrity by systematically providing source references, citations, and permission statements for all instructional materials reinforces institutional standards of academic honesty for learners. Properly referencing text, media, images, and third-party content—and explicitly demonstrating permission or open-licensing compliance—educates students on intellectual property rights, teaches proper citation formats, and sets a clear standard for scholarly work in the discipline.   
* **Note to Reviewers**: Examine the course to determine whether instructional materials are properly referenced and attributed. Verify that references follow a recognized style guide (e.g., APA, MLA, Chicago) or at minimum include author/owner name, publication date, title, URL/source, and license type. Check that permissions to copy, download, or host digital materials (e.g., downloaded journal PDFs, textbook chapters, copyrighted images, downloaded videos) are posted alongside the material or in a centralized "Permissions" section or syllabus document. A single comprehensive citation suffices when an extensive body of material originates from a single publisher or source. Note that meeting QM standards regarding references and permissions does not guarantee or imply that specific copyright laws of any country are met.   
* **Hybrid Courses**: N/A  
* **Competency-Based Courses**: N/A  
* **Synchronous Courses**: N/A  
* **Aligned Elements**:  
  * **Standardized Source References & Attributions**:   
    * Explicit, properly formatted citations (following APA, MLA, Chicago, or a recognized style guide) for all course materials, including instructor-created content, journal articles, publisher slide decks, videos, audio recordings, tables, and websites.   
    * Inclusion of essential citation components at minimum: author or owner name, publication date, resource title, URL or publisher source, and license type (if openly licensed).  
    * Clear source attributions provided directly alongside or beneath specific instructional items, in course document footers, or consolidated in a dedicated references list.  
  * **Comprehensive Attribution Across Material Types**:  
    * Source references provided for instructor-created materials, lecture slides, text pages, and original graphics.  
    * Attributions attached to images, illustrations, graphics, tables, and non-public domain visual media.  
    * Complete source citations for external videos, audio recordings, podcasts, embedded web pages, and curated websites.  
    * Proper citations for scholarly journal articles, open-source textbooks, and Open Educational Resources (OER).  
  * **Copyright Permissions & Intellectual Property Compliance**:  
    * Explicit permission notices posted in the course alongside materials or in a centralized "Permissions" section (e.g., within the syllabus) when copyright-protected content is posted directly.  
    * Explicit attributions and copyright disclosures for all module images, graphics, and visual media that are not in the public domain.  
    * Documented permissions for downloaded scholarly journal PDFs, converted textbook chapter PDFs, downloaded video files, or instructor-published journal articles posted directly to the LMS.  
    * Clear open-license statements or links to original license terms for openly licensed materials (e.g., Creative Commons).  
    * Reliance on institutional library database access rights or publisher platforms that provide legitimate access permissions for scholarly articles and digital texts.  
  * **Single-Source References & Course-Level Consolidation**:  
    * Single master references provided in the syllabus or a centralized "Course Resources/Permissions" document when an extensive collection of materials originates from a single publisher (e.g., citing "All Lecture Slides provided courtesy of Publisher X").   
    * Consolidated single-reference entries in the syllabus or "Instructional Materials" section when an extensive body of materials originates from a single source (e.g., listing a publisher once for "All PowerPoint Slides").  
    * Single master reference documents or syllabus sections listing attributions for all media assets used across course modules.   
* **Violating Examples**:  
  * Downloading a PDF of a copyrighted scholarly journal article or textbook chapter and uploading it directly into an LMS module without posting proof of copyright permission, licensing rights, or a library database link.  
  * Using decorative or instructional images, charts, and diagrams throughout lecture slides and module pages without providing any source attributions, owner names, or license information.  
  * Embedding third-party videos downloaded from streaming platforms into LMS pages with all creator credentials, titles, and original URLs stripped out.  
  * Utilizing Open Educational Resources (OER) content extensively across the course without including Creative Commons attribution links, license types, or original author credit.

## **Standard 4.4**

* **ID**: GR-S4.4

* **Summary**: The instructional materials represent up-to-date theory and practice in the discipline.  
* **Rubric Type**: Green  
* **Points**: 2pts  
* **Priority Weight**: Medium  
* **Scope of Implementation**: Course-wide, integrated across lesson modules, required/optional readings, media resources, instructional pages, case studies, and lab materials.  
* **Passing Threshold**: 85% of total examined instructional materials present in the course represent up-to-date theory, current research, and modern industry/academic practice in the discipline (or foundational historical sources properly contextualized for current application).  
* **Annotation**: The instructional materials represent current thinking, practice, and application in the discipline. An introductory computer course might include information on recent trends in data storage. A writing course might provide learners with examples of visual composition for analysis. A chemistry course might include computerized models to demonstrate chemical operations. An early childhood education course might include evidence-based practices for inclusive learning spaces. Decisions on whether the course meets this Specific Review Standard may be difficult for reviewers whose expertise is not in the course discipline. Consult with the team Subject Matter Expert and use professional judgment to determine if the materials are current. Older works considered foundational may meet Specific Review Standard 4.4. The Subject Matter Expert on the team verifies that a work is foundational in the discipline. Foundational sources may be given appropriate context and application for current practice. For example, a science course contains instructional materials that present current theory and practice, and the course may also include materials of historical significance. In considering whether this Specific Review Standard is met, examine the Course Worksheet for additional information regarding the currency and selection of instructional materials.   
* **Rationales**: Ensuring that instructional materials reflect current thinking, contemporary research, and modern application in the discipline equips learners with relevant, actionable knowledge aligned with modern professional and academic standards. When courses integrate up-to-date materials—or frame foundational historical sources within current practice—they prevent the dissemination of obsolete information, foster accurate conceptual understanding, and better prepare students for real-world application.

Consult with the team Subject Matter Expert (SME) and use professional judgment if evaluating materials outside your primary discipline. Review the Course Worksheet for context on material currency and selection. Note that older works considered foundational may meet this standard if verified as foundational in the discipline by the SME and provided with appropriate context showing their relevance to current practice.   
* **Hybrid Courses**: N/A  
* **Competency-Based Courses**: N/A  
* **Synchronous Courses**: N/A  
* **Aligned Elements**:  
  * **Current Disciplinary Thinking & Contemporary Practice**:  
    * Instructional materials (textbooks, articles, multimedia, web resources) that reflect modern theories, contemporary research, and current industry or academic standards.   
    * Inclusion of updated technological tools, modern technical applications, or recent developments in the field (e.g., covering recent data storage trends in an introductory computer science course).  
    * Contemporary instructional media and visual examples reflecting current communication and design practices (e.g., visual composition examples in a writing course).  
    * Modern computational tools and digital simulations utilized to demonstrate discipline operations (e.g., computerized modeling software in a chemistry course).  
    * Incorporation of current evidence-based practices and contemporary professional methodologies (e.g., evidence-based practices for inclusive learning spaces in early childhood education).  
  * **Foundational Works & Historical Contextualization**:  
    * Inclusion of older, foundational works verified as seminal by a Subject Matter Expert, presented alongside appropriate context explaining their historical significance and connection to current practice.

  * A healthcare or nursing course includes clinical practice guidelines that have been superseded by newer evidence-based procedures, without providing updated materials or notes explaining current standards.

## **Standard 4.5**

* **ID**: GR-S4.5  
* **Category**: Instructional Materials  
* **Summary**: A variety of instructional materials is used in the course.  
* **Rubric Type**: Green  
* **Points**: 2pts  
* **Priority Weight**: Medium  
* **Scope of Implementation**: Course-wide, integrated across lesson modules, reading lists, media playlists, case studies, and instructional resource pages.  
* **Passing Threshold**: Minimum 1 out of the 3 designated types of variety present in the course: (1) a variety of media/material types (e.g., text, video, podcast, websites); (2) different perspectives and representations of ideas; OR (3) representations of a variety of voices, backgrounds, and cultural perspectives.  
* **Annotation**: The course presents a variety of relevant instructional materials that may include textbooks and other publications, instructor-created resources, websites, and multimedia. Variety may take the form of different types of media used to deliver content. Variety may take the form of different perspectives and different representations of ideas. Variety may also appear in images representing different types of people. Examples, questions, and case studies use names from a wide variety of cultures and represent different interpersonal dynamics. Variety does not refer to a specific number, but rather refers to using different types and qualities of aligned material in the course. The value of variety in instructional materials is supported by research-based frameworks and pedagogies including Universal Design for Learning (UDL). Universal Design for Learning guidelines promote the perspective that diverse learners benefit from multiple means of representation in their learning materials. Quality instructional materials reflect the USF student population who use them and thereby help learners feel included and a sense of belonging. Equitable representation and experiences in instructional materials will help foster learner engagement.   
* **Rationales**: Incorporating a variety of instructional materials aligns with Universal Design for Learning (UDL) frameworks by providing multiple means of representation to support diverse learning needs. Presenting distinct media formats, opposing academic perspectives, and diverse voices—including underrepresented scholars, varied cultural backgrounds, and inclusive scenarios—ensures that course content reflects student demographics, fosters a strong sense of inclusion and belonging, and enhances overall learner engagement.   
* **Note to Reviewers**: Confirm that at least one out of three types of "variety" are met to determine whether this Specific Review Standard is marked as "Met": (1) variety of instructional material types, (2) different perspectives/ideas, or (3) representation of a variety of voices and backgrounds. Decisions on this standard may be difficult for reviewers whose expertise is not in the course discipline; check the Course Worksheet, consult with the team Subject Matter Expert (SME), and use professional judgment. Reviewers may provide helpful recommendations related to any qualities of "variety," even if the standard is met.   
* **Hybrid Courses**: N/A  
* **Competency-Based Courses**: N/A  
* **Synchronous Courses**: N/A  
* **Aligned Elements**:  
  * **Variety of Material Types & Media Formats (Variety Type 1\)**:  
    * Combination of textual and multimedia delivery formats (e.g., textbook chapters, instructor-created audio podcasts, topical videos, scholarly journal articles, and curated web links).  
    * Options for learners on how they consume content (e.g., reading an article, viewing a video, or listening to a podcast).  
    * Integration of varied instructional media assets, such as topical video series, audio recordings, podcasts, and slide presentations alongside reading materials.  
  * **Diverse Perspectives & Representations of Ideas (Variety Type 2\)**:  
    * Graphic organizers or conceptual maps outlining distinct, contrasting perspectives offered within lesson materials.  
    * Assigned reading lists identifying thinkers who take opposing or alternative approaches on debatable discipline topics.  
    * Inclusion of course texts and resources by authors who challenge the status quo or represent viewpoints traditionally underrepresented or omitted in the discipline.  
  * **Inclusive Voices, Cultural Diversity, & Universal Design (Variety Type 3\)**:  
    * Visual media, images, graphics, and videos representing a wide variety of cultures, countries, physical abilities, backgrounds, and interpersonal dynamics.  
    * Case studies, practice scenarios, and assignment prompts that describe diverse family structures, varied interpersonal dynamics, and names/stories from multiple cultures.   
    * Alignment with Universal Design for Learning (UDL) guidelines by offering multiple means of representation to foster student engagement, equity, inclusion, and a sense of belonging.  
    * Instructional videos representing a variety of voices and perspectives.   
* **Violating Examples**:  
  * A course relies solely on text reading assignments from a single textbook across all modules, offering no videos, podcasts, web resources, or alternative media formats.  
  * All case studies, examples, and practice problems in a course use culturally homogeneous names, traditional family structures, and uniform demographics without any cultural or interpersonal diversity.  
  * Instructional materials present a controversial or debatable disciplinary topic strictly from one rigid viewpoint, omitting opposing theories, critical perspectives, or underrepresented scholars.

## **Standard 4.6**

* **ID**: GO-S4.6

* **Annotation**: Activities encourage students to critically self-reflect on their preconceptions about content and/or the instructor provides a platform for discussion around preconceptions.   
* **Rationales**: Encouraging learners to critically examine their existing assumptions, cultural biases, and prior knowledge creates a foundation for deeper cognitive engagement and transformative learning. When students evaluate course topics through multiple lenses and reflect on how their preconceptions shape their understanding, they become more open to new perspectives, refine their analytical thinking, and achieve a more nuanced mastery of the discipline.   
* **Note to Reviewers**: One meaningful, substantive activity or assignment can count toward evidence of this standard being met. Verify that the activity explicitly prompts students to critically self-reflect on their prior knowledge, assumptions, or preconceptions, or provides a dedicated platform/forum for open discussion around those preconceptions.

* **Synchronous Courses**: N/A

  * **Critical Reflection & Preconception Exploration**:  
    * Reflective learning activities explicitly designed to encourage students to critically self-reflect on their existing assumptions, prior knowledge, and initial preconceptions about course content.  
    * Prompts that guide learners to examine personal cultural preconceptions, personal biases, or unexamined beliefs regarding subject matter topics.  
    * Learning platforms or dedicated discussion spaces facilitated by the instructor to foster open dialogue around student preconceptions and evolving perspectives.  
  * **Multi-Perspective & Authentic Scenarios**:  
    * Case studies and scenario analyses where students assess situations from multiple lenses, viewpoints, or cultural frameworks, explicitly incorporating and questioning their own initial perspectives.

# **General Standard 5**

* **Category**: Learning Activities and Learner Interaction  
* **General Standard Summary**: Learning activities foster and facilitate learner interaction and engagement.  
* **General Standard Overview**: Course components that promote active learning contribute to the learning process.

## **Standard 5.1**

* **ID**: GR-S5.1  
* **Category**: Learning Activities and Learner Interaction  
* **Summary**: The learning activities help learners achieve the stated objectives.  
* **Rubric Type**: Green  
* **Points**: 3pts  
* **Priority Weight**: High  
* **Scope of Implementation**: Course-wide, across all lesson modules, learning activities, practice assignments, discussion forums, lab exercises, and assignment drafting phases.  
* **Passing Threshold**: 85% of total examined learning activities present in the course must align with and directly support course-level or module/unit-level learning outcomes by providing active practice, discovery, and trial-and-error opportunities.  
* **Annotation**: The purpose of learning activities is to provide opportunities for learners to practice their new skills and knowledge through interaction with the course content, other learners, and their instructor. Such interactions help learners achieve the stated learning outcomes. Learning activities align with course and module/unit-level learning outcomes (2.1 and 2.2), help to prepare learners for the assessments (3.1), and utilize instructional materials (4.1) and course tools (6.1) to engage learners in activities that promote achievement of the stated learning outcomes. Learning activities provide reinforcement and promote achievement through interaction with the course content and learning community. An “activity” is any assigned course activity that allows students to practice using the new knowledge and skills they are learning in the course. Activities allow for practice, discovery, and trial and error.   
* **Rationales**: Learning activities serve as the essential bridge between instructional content and high-stakes assessment, offering structured opportunities for learners to practice new skills, discover concepts, and learn through trial-and-error. When learning activities align directly with course-level and module-level objectives—and utilize instructional materials and tools effectively—they actively engage students, reinforce core knowledge through interaction, and drive measurable learning achievement.   
* **Note to Reviewers**: Ensure that the learning activities effectively help students achieve the course-level and module/unit-level learning outcomes. Confirm that assigned activities provide authentic opportunities for practice, discovery, and trial-and-error directly relevant to the target skills. Verify that activities prepare learners for assessments (3.1) and utilize materials (4.1) and tools (6.1). Special Situations: In cases where course-level learning outcomes are institutionally mandated and cannot be modified by the instructor (check the Course Worksheet), evaluate alignment against the module/unit-level outcomes instead.   
* **Hybrid Courses**: Copies of or information about activities completed during live, face-to-face sessions are listed and documented on the course site.   
* **Competency-Based Courses**: N/A  
* **Synchronous Courses**: Copies of or information about activities completed during live, real-time sessions are listed and documented on the course site.   
* **Aligned Elements**:  
  * **Formative Practice & Scaffolding Tasks**:  
    * Submission of rough drafts, outlines, or preliminary milestones for major papers, projects, or presentations prior to final evaluation.  
    * Completion of guided lessons, workbooks, practice quizzes, self-checks, and instructional videos with embedded check-in questions to reinforce concepts.  
    * Constructing sample models, financial budgets, or mathematical equations as practice activities prior to graded assessments.  
  * **Interactive & Applied Skill Building**:  
    * Active participation in scenario-based activities, role-playing exercises, virtual field trips, simulation exercises, and web scavenger hunts.  
    * Creating conceptual organizers, such as speech outlines, mind maps, concept maps, and graphic organizers, to synthesize complex information.  
    * Operating lab equipment, conducting hands-on or virtual laboratory skills exercises, preparing chemical solutions, and practicing safety/waste disposal procedures.  
  * **Collaborative Practice & Feedback Loops**:  
    * Brainstorming and receiving instructor feedback on project or speech topics.  
    * Submitting assignment outlines or practice drafts for peer review and critique.  
    * Recording and practicing speeches or presentations prior to final delivery.  
    * Engaging in structured, topic-focused class discussions aligned directly with module learning outcomes.  
* **Violating Examples**:  
  * A module outcome requires students to "deliver a practiced persuasive speech," but the course activities consist solely of reading textbook chapters on public speaking with no opportunity to practice speaking or receive feedback.  
  * A module outcome states students will "prepare an operating budget," but students are only shown completed sample budgets worked out by the instructor without any activity requiring them to construct a budget themselves.  
  * In an advanced nursing course, the module outcome requires students to "describe epidemiological principles in vulnerable populations," but the corresponding learning activity is limited to reading about and discussing general healthcare worker job roles.  
  * A course outcome requires students to "design and conduct scientific experiments and safely use lab equipment," but all module activities are restricted to text-based multiple-choice quizzes without any virtual lab simulations or procedural practice.

## **Standard 5.2**

* **ID**: GR-S5.2  
* **Category**: Learning Activities and Learner Interaction  
* **Summary**: Learning activities provide opportunities for interactions that support active learning.  
* **Rubric Type**: Green  
* **Points**: 3pts  
* **Priority Weight**: High  
* **Scope of Implementation**: Course-wide, across lesson modules, learning activities, assignment prompts, discussion forums, interactive exercises, and real-time session agendas.  
* **Passing Threshold**: 85% of total examined learning activities present in the course must provide meaningful interaction opportunities that support active learning (taking into account whether learner-learner interaction is applicable as indicated on the Course Worksheet).  
* **Annotation**: Interactive learning activities promote active learning and engagement through three types of interaction: learner-content, learner-instructor, and learner-learner. (The course does not have to include learner-learner interaction for this Specific Review Standard to be met. Refer to the Course Worksheet to determine whether learner-learner interaction is suitable for the course.) Meaningful interactions that promote learners’ development of skills are designed as activities to support the course outcomes and may vary with the subject matter, purpose, and level of the course. Look for the purpose of the interactions and not just the number of opportunities for interaction. Active learning involves learners engaging by "doing" something, such as analyzing data, evaluating potential solutions to a problem, or applying concepts and information. Fostering active learning entails guiding learners to increasing levels of responsibility for their own learning through interaction opportunities that reinforce learners’ confidence, discovery through experience, and engaging in reflection. Active learning opportunities help students reflect on their understanding by encouraging them to make connections between their prior knowledge and new concepts. Opportunities for active learning reflect the variety of learners’ characteristics, backgrounds, and abilities. Activities designed to ensure equity in learning experiences may include but are not limited to offering a choice of activities where possible, giving learners autonomy over their role in collaborative work, and providing learners opportunities to apply their own experiences and prior knowledge to solve real-world problems.   
* **Rationales**: Interactive learning activities foster active learning by shifting students from passive consumers to active participants who engage by "doing"—analyzing data, applying concepts, and evaluating solutions. Guided interaction opportunities strengthen learner confidence, encourage critical reflection, and help students connect prior knowledge with new concepts. Furthermore, incorporating equity-focused choice and autonomy across interactions accommodates diverse learner backgrounds and promotes self-directed, experiential learning.   
* **Note to Reviewers**: Review the types of interactions designed into the activities based on the nature of the course and not on personal preferences. Determine if opportunities for learner-instructor and learner-content interaction are present in the course. If learner-learner interaction is appropriate to the course (check the Course Worksheet), it is also included in the course. Look for the underlying purpose and pedagogical quality of the interactions rather than merely counting the total number of opportunities. Keep in mind that learning environments usually are broader than a single course and may include informal networks that are beyond the scope of a QM review. Continuing Education Courses: In self-paced courses, learner-instructor interaction may be limited or non-existent; if the Course Worksheet indicates it is not facilitated, an exception is made and this standard is marked "Met."   
* **Hybrid Courses**: In courses that use both online and face-to-face settings, the learning activities that occur in these two settings are connected by a common thread or theme and are mutually reinforcing. The connection and reinforcement are made clear to learners (e.g., sequencing different parts of an activity in an alternating pattern between online and real-time meetings). The course provides copies of or information about activities to be completed during real-time meetings.   
* **Competency-Based Courses**: In competency-based courses, interaction with the instructor, facilitator, coach, mentor, or assessor may take different forms. Learner-learner interaction in discussion forums is encouraged but may be limited by the differential pace of individual learners’ progress through the course; other forms of learner-learner interaction may be impractical as learners prepare in different ways for assessment. Communication through program-level, learner-learner forums is a viable alternative to course-based forums.   
* **Synchronous Courses**: In courses that use both online and real-time settings, the learning activities that occur in these settings are connected by a common thread or theme and are mutually reinforcing. The connection and reinforcement are made clear to learners (e.g., sequencing activity stages across online and synchronous meetings). The course provides copies of or information about activities to be completed during real-time meetings.   
* **Aligned Elements**:  
  * **Learner-Instructor Interaction Activities**:  
    * Assignments or projects submitted for direct instructor feedback, including audiovisual assignment feedback or personalized messaging.  
    * Learner-instructor exchanges in synchronous web sessions, asynchronous discussion boards, or instructor-moderated FAQ discussion forums.  
    * Instructor-driven polls, video announcements, and study/review sessions where learners bring remaining questions from practice quizzes.  
  * **Learner-Content Interaction Activities**:  
    * Active engagement prompts during media consumption, such as responding to guided questions while watching a recorded lecture.  
    * Completion of interactive online exercises, workbooks, concept mapping, graphic organizing, or virtual expeditions.  
    * Choice of learning content selected from a provided set of aligned options.  
  * **Learner-Learner Interaction Activities** (when appropriate per Course Worksheet):  
    * Collaborative group discussions, small-group projects, and team problem-solving assignments.  
    * Structured peer critiques, think-pair-share exercises, role-playing activities, and collaborative peer lessons.  
    * LMS-based discussion groups evaluating disciplinary challenges, collaboratively developing summaries, and presenting group conclusions for class feedback.  
  * **Equity & Active Learning Provisions**:  
    * Activities designed to promote equity by offering choice among aligned learning options, giving learners autonomy over their role in collaborative tasks, and providing opportunities to apply personal prior knowledge to solve authentic problems.  
* **Violating Examples**:  
  * A course consisting entirely of passive video watching and static reading assignments without any guided questions, self-check exercises, or active application tasks (lacking active learner-content interaction).  
  * A course where student assignments receive only automated numerical grades without qualitative feedback, Q\&A forums, or active communication channels with the instructor (lacking learner-instructor interaction).  
  * A hybrid course where online assignments and face-to-face class sessions cover completely unrelated topics without a unifying theme, logical sequence, or clear connection explaining how they reinforce each other.  
  * A course that mandates complex synchronous group assignments despite the Course Worksheet explicitly identifying the course as self-paced continuing education where learner-learner interaction is inappropriate.

## **Standard 5.3**

* **ID**: GR-S5.3  
* **Category**: Learning Activities and Learner Interaction  
* **Summary**: The instructor's plan for regular interaction with learners in substantive ways during the course is clearly stated.  
* **Rubric Type**: Green  
* **Points**: 3pts  
* **Priority Weight**: High  
* **Scope of Implementation**: Course syllabus, course orientation / "Start Here" module, Course Expectations page, office hours section, assignment submission guidelines, and communication channels throughout the course.  
* **Passing Threshold**: Minimum 1 instance of a comprehensive instructor interaction plan clearly stated in the course (e.g., in the syllabus, "Start Here" module, or Course Expectations page) that explicitly defines BOTH specified regular time intervals (turnaround times for emails, Q\&A, assignments) AND the nature of substantive feedback/direct instruction across course activities (taking into account any self-paced/continuing education exceptions noted in the Course Worksheet).  
* **Annotation**: A clear plan for instructor-learner interaction helps promote substantive interaction between instructors and learners during the course. The plan includes details regarding when learners can expect the instructor's responses to questions, at what intervals learners will receive substantive instructor feedback on assignments, and how the instructor has designed other regular feedback and communication opportunities into the course. Frequent feedback from the instructor increases learners' sense of engagement in a course. Learners are better able to manage their learning activities when they know upfront when to expect feedback from the instructor. Clear information is provided about when learners will receive instructor responses to emails and posted questions, feedback on assignments, and grades. For example, instructors might state that they will reply to emails within 24 hours, and feedback for assignments will be posted within a week after the due date. This information may appear in the course syllabus or in a “Start Here” folder. If it is necessary to alter the response-time plan during the course, how the adjustment will be made is clearly communicated.   
* **Rationales**: Providing a clear instructor interaction plan establishes predictable communication expectations, increasing learners' sense of engagement and enabling them to plan and manage their learning activities effectively. Stating explicit response times for inquiries and grade turnarounds reduces student anxiety, while committing to regular, substantive feedback (such as individualized rubric comments, direct instruction, and targeted intervention for knowledge gaps) deepens learning, supports diverse accessibility needs, and builds a supportive instructor-learner relationship.   
* **Note to Reviewers**: Look for an instructor plan for interacting with learners that provides substantive feedback at regular intervals. The instructor plan must include BOTH specified regular intervals and substantive interaction for this Specific Review Standard to be marked “Met.” Verify that response times for emails, posted questions, assignment feedback, and grades are clearly stated (e.g., email replies within 24–48 hours, assignment grading within 7 days). Check that the plan outlines how substantive feedback will be delivered (e.g., rubric comments, audio/text options, addressing learning gaps). Note that meeting this standard does not guarantee or imply compliance with external country or federal regulations regarding Regular and Substantive Interaction (RSI).   
* **Hybrid Courses**: In addition to the online interaction plan, the instructor’s plan for real-time interaction—such as providing direct instruction, facilitating live discussions, and answering questions in person or during live web sessions—is clearly stated on the course site.   
* **Competency-Based Courses**: Interaction with the instructor, facilitator, coach, mentor, or assessor may take different forms. The course introduction indicates the various forms of faculty and staff support available to the learner and explicitly explains when learners can expect to receive feedback.   
* **Synchronous Courses**: In addition to the online interaction plan, the instructor’s plan for real-time interaction—such as providing direct instruction, live facilitation, and answering questions during synchronous sessions—is clearly stated on the course site.   
* **Aligned Elements**:  
  * **Defined Communication Timelines & Turnaround Plans**:  
    * Explicit response timeframes for instructor communication (e.g., replying to emails or Q\&A discussion posts within 24–48 hours).  
    * Specified turnarounds for grading and returning substantive assignment feedback (e.g., within 7 days of the assignment due date).  
    * Clear protocols explaining how adjustments to the response-time plan will be communicated to learners if unexpected delays occur.  
  * **Plans for Regular Instructor-Learner Touchpoints**:  
    * Scheduled office/learner hours (virtual or in-person) with direct access options.  
    * Routine posting of course announcements, weekly overview messages, or mid-term email check-ins.  
    * Individualized touchpoints, such as instructor responses to self-reflective learning activities, intro discussions, or progress check-ins.  
    * Schedules for online review sessions, live webinars, or pre-exam Q\&A meetings.  
  * **Substantive Feedback & Remediation Frameworks**:  
    * Detailed plans for providing individualized coursework feedback (e.g., detailed rubric comments, summary notes, in-text annotations, or video/audio feedback).  
    * Strategies for addressing identified gaps in learner preparation or comprehension to ensure all learners acquire essential background context.  
    * Offers to provide feedback in accessible alternative formats (e.g., providing audio recordings with written transcripts upon request).  
    * Clear guidelines on instructor availability to answer questions regarding course content and complex concepts.  
  * **Modality-Specific Support Declarations**:  
    * Statements in hybrid/synchronous courses detailing real-time direct instruction and live Q\&A plans.  
    * Statements in competency-based courses outlining available roles (instructors, coaches, mentors, assessors) and feedback response schedules.  
    * Verification in non-facilitated self-paced continuing education courses that learner-instructor interaction is limited or non-existent per Course Worksheet guidelines.  
* **Violating Examples**:  
  * The syllabus mentions that the instructor will grade assignments and answer emails, but provides no specific timelines, turnarounds, or intervals (e.g., "I will reply when possible" or "grades will be posted periodically").  
  * The course states that emails will be answered within 24 hours, but lacks any description or plan for providing substantive feedback on student assignments, offering only raw numerical points without qualitative comments or guidance.  
  * A synchronous or hybrid course provides a meeting schedule, but omits any statement outlining the instructor's plan for real-time direct instruction, live interaction, or handling student questions during live sessions.  
  * A competency-based course lists multiple support roles (coaches, mentors, assessors) but fails to explain when or how learners can expect to receive feedback from them on their performance assessments.

## **Standard 5.4**

* **ID**: GR-S5.4  
* **Category**: Learning Activities and Learner Interaction  
* **Summary**: The requirements for learner interaction are clearly stated.  
* **Rubric Type**: Green  
* **Points**: 2pts  
* **Priority Weight**: Medium  
* **Scope of Implementation**: Course syllabus, course information/orientation module, discussion forum prompts, group project assignment instructions, grading rubrics, and real-time session agendas.  
* **Passing Threshold**: 85% of total required course interactions (discussions, group projects, peer reviews, instructor-led exchanges, and synchronous sessions) must feature explicitly stated expectations for frequency, quantity, quality, and timeliness (taking into account any self-paced/continuing education exceptions noted in the Course Worksheet).  
* **Annotation**: A clear explanation of the requirements for learner interaction helps learners plan and manage their class participation and is important for promoting learners’ active involvement in the course. The statement of requirements also provides a basis for the instructor to evaluate learner participation. Guidelines or expectations for learners interacting with their instructor are stated clearly, including if learner responses to instructor-initiated interaction are required. Expectations for learner participation may be stated in the course information page or syllabus. These requirements may specify the nature of the required participation and expectations for frequency and quality of the learner’s interactions. Learners might have a frequency expectation that requires them to log into the course site, or complete specific tasks in the site, a certain number of days per week. More specific, task-related performance expectations may be included in the individual task description. Learner differences in the context of course interactions can present both challenges and opportunities. In some instances, a learner’s interaction with the instructor, with content, or with fellow learners may entail cultural differences or personal sensitivities related to the nature of the interaction. The instructor can alert learners to sensitive content with descriptions that identify potentially problematic or traumatizing content in chosen instructional materials. Clearly explaining the role of the instructor and expectations for interactions with the instructor and with other learners is especially helpful to learners from cultures in which deference to the instructor is customary and who may need encouragement to actively participate by voicing their opinions. Interaction policies might also include statements related to belonging as well as bullying and microaggressions.  
* **Rationales**:Clearly articulating interaction requirements provides learners with explicit expectations regarding the frequency, timeliness, quantity, and quality of their participation. This enables students to manage their time effectively, actively engage in learning activities, and understand how their contributions will be evaluated. Clear guidelines also address cultural differences, foster an inclusive environment of belonging, establish netiquette regarding sensitive topics, and mitigate microaggressions or communication barriers.   
* **Note to Reviewers**: Look for a clear statement of the instructor’s expectations for learner participation in required course interactions (frequency, length, timeliness, quality, etc.), including learner-learner and learner-instructor interactions in assessments and activities, if relevant. (Check the Course Worksheet to determine if learner-learner interaction is a component of this course.) Verify that criteria specify quantity (e.g., word count, posting days per week) and quality (e.g., substantive evaluation vs. simple summary) as well as evaluation mechanisms like rubrics (see Standard 3.3). Special Situations: In self-paced continuing education courses where learner-learner interaction is not appropriate, an exception is made and this standard is marked "Met."   
* **Hybrid Courses**: Learner interaction requirements for real-time course sessions are posted in the course site. Look for a clear statement of expectations for interacting with peers live, such as guidelines for in-person group work for hybrid courses. These courses might also have separate requirements for attendance and participation in real-time course sessions.  
* **Competency-Based Courses**: N/A  
* **Synchronous Courses**: Learner interaction requirements for real-time course sessions are posted in the course site. Look for a clear statement of expectations for interacting with peers live, such as guidelines for breakout rooms and other collaborative activities in synchronous courses. These courses might also have separate requirements for attendance and participation in real-time course sessions.  
* **Aligned Elements**:  
  * **Frequency, Login, & Timelines Expectations**:  
    * Stated expectations for course login frequency (e.g., logging into the LMS site at least 3 days per week).  
    * Explicit submission schedules defining deadlines for initial discussion posts and reply posts to peers or the instructor.  
  * **Quantitative & Qualitative Participation Criteria**:  
    * Clear definitions of quantity and quality in task descriptions (e.g., specifying that a discussion post must be at least 250 words, present original analysis, and offer an evaluation rather than a simple summary).  
    * Grading rubrics detailing how learner-learner and learner-instructor interactions are evaluated (aligned with Standard 3.3).  
  * **Instructor Interaction & Response Guidelines**:  
    * Stated guidelines for interacting with the instructor, including whether learner responses to instructor-initiated posts or communications are required.  
    * Explicit descriptions of the instructor’s role in discussions, office hours, and feedback loops.  
  * **Inclusive Interaction Policies & Cultural Sensitivity**:  
    * Guidelines recognizing cultural differences, encouraging active participation from learners accustomed to instructor deference, and establishing expectations around belonging, bullying, and microaggressions.  
    * Content advisories or descriptions alerting learners to sensitive, potentially problematic, or traumatizing topics in course interactions or materials (aligned with Standard 1.3).  
  * **Task-Specific Group & Collaboration Rules**:  
    * Task-level performance guidelines for group assignments, specifying individual participation expectations and response turnaround times.  
  * **Real-Time Session Expectations (Hybrid/Synchronous)**:  
    * Clear statements governing live interaction in hybrid or synchronous settings, including attendance policies, breakout room collaboration protocols, and real-time peer engagement rules.  
* **Violating Examples**:  
  * A weekly discussion forum assignment states "participate in the discussion board this week" without defining required post length, number of peer replies, submission deadlines, or quality criteria.  
  * A group project assignment requires students to "work together to complete a report" but lacks individual participation expectations, peer contribution guidelines, or rubrics evaluating team interaction.  
  * A synchronous course requires live attendance in web sessions but provides no guidelines regarding how students should interact during breakout rooms or contribute during live discussions.  
  * Course materials cover highly sensitive or controversial topics, but the course lacks any interaction guidelines, netiquette policies, or microaggression expectations to maintain respectful dialogue.

## **Standard 5.5**

* **ID**: GO-S5.5  
* **Category**: Learning Activities and Learner Interaction  
* **Summary**: Course contains resources or activities intended to build a sense of class community, support open communication, and establish trust.  
* **Rubric Type**: Gold  
* **Points**: 2pts  
* **Priority Weight**: Medium  
* **Scope of Implementation**: Course orientation / "Start Here" module, lesson modules, discussion forums, Q\&A spaces, peer review activities, group assignments, and synchronous session agendas placed throughout the course.  
* **Passing Threshold**: Presence of community-building resources or activities representing at least 2 out of the 3 designated categories (Social, Cognitive, and "Getting Started") integrated intentionally throughout the course.  
* **Annotation**: Building a sense of community mitigates the solitude and isolation reported by online learners (Bibeau, 2001). Courses that promote class community help learning occur “in a social context” (Dewey) and mitigate the perception of a correspondence course. Activities that build class community early on in the course typically fall into three categories: Social activities which focus on self-expression; Cognitive activities which focus on academic and professional goals; “Getting Started” activities which familiarize learners with course materials and technology. Each of these types of activities foster social presence, promote learner engagement and open up avenues for communication. Social presence involves affective expression, open communication, and group cohesion. Each of these factors promote learner engagement in an online course (Annand, 2011). Look for answers to these questions when developing these types of community activities: Is the activity non-threatening? Is it learner focused (social)? Is it content focused (cognitive)? Does it require learners to read and respond to each other? Does it encourage learners to find something in common with other learners? Does it require learners to be reflective?   
* **Rationales**: Building a strong sense of class community mitigates the feelings of isolation and solitude often experienced by online learners, ensuring learning occurs within a supportive social context rather than an isolated correspondence model. Fostering social presence—through affective expression, open communication, and group cohesion—establishes trust, encourages peer engagement, and opens enduring avenues for meaningful communication and reflection throughout the learning experience.   
* **Note to Reviewers**:Look for evidence that at least 2 out of the 3 categories (Social activities focusing on self-expression, Cognitive activities focusing on academic/professional goals, and "Getting Started" activities familiarizing learners with materials and technology) are present in the course. Interactions must be placed throughout the course rather than limited to the first week. Evaluate whether activities are non-threatening, require learners to read and respond to one another, encourage finding common ground, and foster reflective dialogue.   
* **Hybrid Courses**: N/A  
* **Competency-Based Courses**: N/A  
* **Synchronous Courses**: For courses with synchronous sessions, part of the session agenda must be specifically dedicated to discussion, peer collaboration, or peer-to-peer active learning experiences that build community.   
* **Aligned Elements**:  
  * **Social & Self-Expression Activities**:  
    * Learner introduction discussions that require meaningful engagement beyond basic statements (e.g., sharing personal interests, experiences, or creative icebreakers and finding commonalities with peers).  
    * Planned informal community events, digital lounge spaces, or study/assignment sessions offered beyond standard instructor office hours.  
  * **Cognitive & Professional Goal Activities**:  
    * Discussion prompts and learning tasks focused on academic objectives, professional aspirations, and reflective self-analysis.  
    * Collaborative group work and active peer review activities supported by explicit response and collaboration guidelines.  
  * **"Getting Started" & Orientation Activities**:  
    * Orientation activities designed to familiarize learners with course navigation, materials, technology tools, and peer communication channels.  
  * **Supportive & Open Communication Channels**:  
    * Dedicated Q\&A question forums (e.g., "Raise Your Hand" or "Ask the Class" discussion boards) that emphasize peer support, community building, and collaborative problem-solving.  
  * **Synchronous Community Integration**:  
    * Synchronous live session agendas that explicitly carve out time for peer-to-peer discussion, active learning, and group collaboration.  
* **Violating Examples**:  
  * The course includes only a single, optional 1-line intro post in Week 1 with no peer response requirements, and no subsequent community-building activities are provided for the rest of the term.  
  * All discussion forums across the course are structured as isolated "post-and-run" tasks where students are prohibited or not required to read, reply to, or engage with classmates' contributions.  
  * The course lacks any Q\&A discussion board, peer support space, or collaborative study channel, forcing learners to troubleshoot technical and academic issues entirely in isolation.  
  * Course activities focus strictly on individual, independent work without any peer review, group collaboration, or opportunities for learners to discuss shared academic and professional goals.

## **Standard 5.6**

* **ID**: GO-S5.6  
* **Category**: Learning Activities and Learner Interaction  
* **Summary**: Course provides activities that emulate real world applications of the discipline, such as experiential learning, case studies, and problem-based activities.  
* **Rubric Type**: Gold  
* **Points**: 2pts  
* **Priority Weight**: Medium  
* **Scope of Implementation**: Lesson modules, assignments, discussion forums, lab preparation, and major authentic projects across the course.  
* **Passing Threshold**: Minimum 1 instance (or explicit inclusion across major course modules) of an authentic learning activity, case study, experiential learning task, or problem-based scenario that emulates real-world discipline applications.  
* **Annotation**: Experiential learning, case studies, and problem-based activities are designed to immerse learners in real world scenarios, with the goal of having learners build on their existing knowledge and skills to analyze specific problems and find solutions. These activities engage learners by having them establish what they know and don’t know, work together to come up with real-world solutions, share those solutions, and review possible results. According to Kolb (1984), experiential learning relies on four elements: Experience; Critical reflection; Abstract conceptualization; and Active experimentation in a new situation. Through experience, online learners are led to make observations and reflections. From there, abstract concepts are explored through critical reflection, which learners can then actively test and evaluate. This process engages the learners in scaffolding what they already know, and creating new knowledge.   
* **Rationales**: Experiential learning, case studies, and problem-based activities immerse learners in authentic, real-world discipline scenarios, bridging the gap between abstract theory and practical execution. Grounded in Kolb's (1984) experiential learning framework—which integrates concrete experience, critical reflection, abstract conceptualization, and active experimentation—these activities empower learners to analyze complex problems, collaborate on real-world solutions, evaluate potential results, and construct new knowledge on existing foundations.   
* **Note to Reviewers**: Look for learning activities and assignments that explicitly emulate real-world discipline practices and scenarios rather than relying solely on abstract memory recall or rote exercises. Verify that activities lead learners through authentic problem-solving processes—identifying what they know and don't know, analyzing mini-cases or scenarios, testing solutions, and critically reflecting on outcomes. Check for portfolio projects, scenario-based discussions, role-playing, pre-lab mini-cases, or debates appropriate to the field.   
* **Hybrid Courses**: N/A  
* **Competency-Based Courses**: N/A  
* **Synchronous Courses**: N/A  
* **Aligned Elements**:  
  * **Portfolio & Authentic Capstone Projects**:  
    * Portfolio projects requiring learners to compile and refine professional, authentic artifacts demonstrating mastery of real-world discipline skills.  
  * **Scenario & Case Study Discussions**:  
    * Scenario-based or case study-driven discussion forums where learners analyze authentic, real-world problems, identify knowledge gaps, evaluate potential solutions, and debate outcomes.  
  * **Role-Playing & Experiential Simulations**:  
    * Interactive role-playing activities that require learners to adopt professional roles and make decisions within simulated workplace or disciplinary scenarios.  
    * Experiment testing, recreation, and virtual laboratory activities that mirror professional investigative procedures.  
  * **Communication & Disciplinary Debates**:  
    * Structured presentations or academic/professional debates tailored to fields where oral or written communication is a core professional competency.  
  * **Pre-Lab Mini-Cases & Risk Analysis**:  
    * Mini-cases integrated as pre-lab work where learners analyze potential errors, safety hazards, or operational failures before engaging in online lab activities.  
* **Violating Examples**:  
  * A course where all assessments consist exclusively of multiple-choice quizzes testing memorized textbook definitions without any authentic applications, case studies, or problem-solving scenarios.  
  * A business or healthcare course that relies entirely on abstract, theoretical lectures without presenting real-world case studies, workplace scenarios, or simulated decision-making exercises.  
  * A communication or public relations course where students only read about messaging strategies but are never asked to draft press releases, deliver presentations, engage in debates, or handle simulated crisis communications.  
  * An online lab course where students follow rigid, step-by-step instructions without any pre-lab mini-cases, problem-based troubleshooting, or critical reflection on potential experimental failures.

## **Standard 5.7**

* **ID**: GO-S5.7  
* **Category**: Learning Activities and Learner Interaction  
* **Summary**: Learners have multiple opportunities to get to know the instructor.  
* **Rubric Type**: Gold  
* **Points**: 2pts  
* **Priority Weight**: Medium  
* **Scope of Implementation**: Course orientation / "Start Here" module, syllabus, weekly module intros, instructional content pages, lecture videos, discussion forums, announcements, virtual office hours, and synchronous/hybrid agendas.  
* **Passing Threshold**: Multiple instances (a formal instructor introduction page/video PLUS at least one or more ongoing touchpoints throughout the course, such as module overview videos, contextualized lecture materials, personal narratives, structured announcements, or active discussion feedback).  
* **Annotation**: Social presence is the ability of learners to project their personal characteristics into the community of inquiry, thereby presenting themselves as ‘real people.’ (Garrison, Anderson, & Archer, 2001). Social presence relies on establishing a welcoming online learning space, as well as acknowledging each individual learner as a valued member of the learning community. When learners understand the background of their instructor, the “distance” between instructor/learners is mitigated. The tone and approach of the instructor in regard to self-introduction will serve as a model for learners. It is important that learners feel the instructor is easily accessible, and willing to communicate consistently throughout the course. Instructors who share personal narratives make a lasting impression on online learners (Aragon, 2003). These personal narratives humanize the instructor, and provide credibility and history to support instructor expertise.   
* **Rationales**: Establishing strong social presence humanizes the instructor, reduces psychological and transactional distance between instructor and learners, and models expected social and academic communication. When instructors share personal narratives, professional backgrounds, and contextualized real-world experiences across multiple touchpoints, it builds credibility, demonstrates accessibility, and helps learners feel valued within a welcoming, supportive online learning community.   
* **Note to Reviewers**: The instructor introduction page can count towards evidence of this standard being met, but should not be the only evidence. Reviewers must look for multiple ongoing opportunities and touchpoints throughout the course where learners get to know the instructor (e.g., module overview videos, contextualized instructional examples, personal narratives, scheduled office hours, structured announcements, stylized instructor avatars, or active feedback in discussions).   
* **Hybrid Courses**: Hybrid course agendas and session materials explicitly outline instructor presence, live role, and structured touchpoints during face-to-face or synchronous components.   
* **Competency-Based Courses**: N/A  
* **Synchronous Courses**: Synchronous session agendas or presentations explicitly document instructor presence, live facilitation roles, personal narrative sharing, and interactive touchpoints during live meetings.   
* **Aligned Elements**:  
  * **Formal Instructor Orientation**:  
    * An instructor introduction page or welcome video in the orientation module sharing professional history, expertise, and personal background.  
  * **Media & Visual Touchpoints**:  
    * Module introduction videos, recorded lecture videos, or podcasts featuring the instructor.  
    * Stylized instructor avatars or graphics that actively reflect instructor personality and brand throughout the course.  
  * **Contextualized Content & Personal Narratives**:  
    * Instructional content across any format that incorporates personal narratives and contextualized professional experiences relating directly to course topics.  
  * **Ongoing Interaction & Communication Plans**:  
    * An asynchronous course announcement plan detailing planned, regular touchpoints and interactions.  
    * An articulated instructor presence plan explaining when and how the instructor engages across modules.  
  * **Direct Engagement & Office Hours**:  
    * Discussion boards where the instructor actively participates and provides direct feedback.  
    * Established virtual office hours with clearly posted dates, times, and access links.  
* **Violating Examples**:  
  * The course contains a brief text-only bio in the syllabus, but the instructor remains completely absent, anonymous, and unrepresented throughout all subsequent lesson modules and videos.  
  * The course relies entirely on generic publisher slide decks and third-party videos, featuring zero video, audio, or written media created by or featuring the instructor.  
  * Discussion forums and assignments receive only automated or generic gradebook comments without any personalized instructor insights, anecdotes, or presence.  
  * Virtual office hours are listed as "by appointment only" with no scheduled check-ins, weekly overview videos, or structured announcement plans for students to connect with the instructor.

## **Standard 5.8**

* **ID**: GO-S5.8  
* **Category**: Learning Activities and Learner Interaction  
* **Summary**: Learners are encouraged to share resources and inject knowledge from different sources of information in their course interactions.  
* **Rubric Type**: Gold  
* **Points**: 2pts  
* **Priority Weight**: Medium  
* **Scope of Implementation**: Lesson modules, discussion forums, collaborative resource boards, group assignments, and synchronous live session agendas across the course.  
* **Passing Threshold**: Minimum 1 instance (or explicit inclusion across multiple learning activities) of a structured prompt or assignment encouraging learners to pull, share, evaluate, and integrate external resources or outside knowledge directly into course interactions.  
* **Annotation**: Teaching presence is the design, facilitation, and direction of cognitive and social processes for the purpose of realizing personally meaningful and educationally worthwhile learning outcomes (Anderson, Rourke, Garrison, & Archer, 2000). Inviting learners to reach out beyond a textbook or assigned readings empowers them to understand a wider scope of research and perspectives. When exposed to different information sources, learners have the opportunity to discern the integrity of those sources and (possibly) share those perceptions with each other. By inviting learners to share resources and add to bring in outside knowledge involves higher order thinking skills, and requires analysis, reflection, and synthesis. Gioia (1987) recommends encouraging learners to become active participants in the classroom by: providing recapitulations and summaries; making observations that integrate concepts and discussions; citing relevant personal examples; asking key questions that lead to revealing discussions; engaging in devil’s advocacy; and disagreeing with the instructor in ways that promote further exploration of the issue. These approaches, although originally posited for the traditional classroom, translate well into the online space. Gioia (1987) also talks about giving learners time to think. In the online space, this translates into providing ample opportunities for reflection and guidance on how to bring that reflection back into online discussions, learning activities, and assignments.   
* **Rationales**: Inviting learners to reach out beyond assigned textbooks or course readings empowers them to explore a wider scope of research and diverse perspectives. Engaging with external information sources fosters higher-order thinking skills—including analysis, reflection, discernment of source integrity, and synthesis—while cultivating an active, participatory learning community where students contribute meaningful insights and real-world connections to course discussions and activities.   
* **Note to Reviewers**: An “introduce yourself” discussion or activity does not count as evidence toward this standard being met. Resources and knowledge shared by the students in interactions should be purposeful and relate directly to content or assignments. Look for structured opportunities, prompts, collaborative sites, or synchronous agendas that explicitly direct learners to retrieve, share, evaluate, and discuss external resources, personal experiences, or outside research.   
* **Hybrid Courses**: N/A  
* **Competency-Based Courses**: N/A  
* **Synchronous Courses**: Synchronous session agendas or presentations must clearly articulate how students will share resources or insights from external sources with one another during live sessions.   
* **Aligned Elements**:  
  * **Purposeful External Resource Sharing**:  
    * Activities, discussions, and collaborative platforms that explicitly require or encourage learners to pull, share, and discuss external, course-related resources to support their arguments and claims.  
  * **Metacognitive & "Explain Your Brain" Activities**:  
    * "Explain your brain" activities where learners are asked to articulate their cognitive thought process, problem-solving strategies, or reflection when tackling a challenge.  
  * **Higher-Order Discussion Facilitation**:  
    * Discussion prompts structured to provide learners reflection time and guidance on bringing external insights, relevant personal/professional examples, or alternative viewpoints back into online interactions.  
    * Opportunities for active participation techniques, such as student-led recapitulations/summaries, observations integrating multiple concepts, asking key revealing questions, engaging in constructive devil's advocacy, or respectfully challenging ideas to deepen exploration.  
  * **Collaborative Knowledge Repositories**:  
    * Shared collaborative spaces (e.g., social bookmarking boards, resource wikis, or curated discussion threads) where learners contribute and critique external industry reports, academic papers, or news articles.  
  * **Synchronous Live Session Integration**:  
    * Synchronous session agendas and slide decks that explicitly plan for and direct student-led sharing of external resources and perspectives during live meetings.  
* **Violating Examples**:  
  * An "Introduce Yourself" icebreaker discussion is the only activity where students share personal information, with no subsequent academic discussions encouraging external resource sharing.  
  * Course discussions strictly restrict students to summarizing assigned textbook chapters, explicitly penalizing or forbidding the introduction of outside articles, real-world examples, or external research.  
  * Discussion prompts ask broad opinion questions that do not require or encourage students to support their claims with external evidence, credible sources, or analytical reflection.  
  * A synchronous live session spent entirely on an instructor lecture with no planned agenda items or interactive structures for students to share outside research or resources with peers.

* **General Standard Summary**: Course technologies support learners' achievement of course outcomes.

## **Standard 6.1**

* **ID**: GR-S6.1  
* **Category**: Course Technology  
* **Summary**: The tools used in the course support the learning objectives.  
* **Rubric Type**: Green  
* **Points**: 3pts  
* **Priority Weight**: High  
* **Scope of Implementation**: Course-wide, across all lesson modules, assessments, learning activities, and instructional materials where tools and software applications are integrated.  
* **Passing Threshold**: 85% of total examined course tools and software applications present in the course must align with and directly support course-level or module/unit-level learning objectives.  
* **Annotation**: The tools selected for the course align with the course-level and module/unit-level outcomes (2.1 and 2.2) by effectively supporting the course’s assessments (3.1), instructional materials (4.1), and learning activities (5.1). Tools are types of software and applications that enable learner interaction and may be used for content delivery or providing feedback in the course; they may be included in or external to the learning management system (LMS). Examples of tools include, but are not limited to: automated self-check exercises, discussion boards, social media, games, whiteboards, blogs, virtual/augmented/extended reality, web conferencing, assignment and quiz tools, video repositories, and collaboration tools. Clear information and instructions are provided regarding how the tools support the learning outcomes. Tools are not used simply for their own sake.   
* **Rationales**: Selecting course tools that align directly with course-level and module-level learning objectives ensures that technology serves a clear pedagogical purpose rather than being used simply for its own sake. When software, applications, and interactive tools effectively support course assessments, instructional materials, and learning activities, learners can focus on mastering content and demonstrating required competencies without being distracted by irrelevant or misaligned technology.   
* **Note to Reviewers**: Examine both course-level and module/unit-level outcomes in your review of tools. Verify that clear information and instructions are provided to learners explaining how the tools support the learning outcomes. Confirm that tools are not used simply for their own sake. Special Situations: In some cases, course outcomes are mandated by the institution, and the individual instructor does not have the authority to change them; in such cases, consider the module/unit-level outcomes to assess whether Specific Review Standard 6.1 is met.   
* **Hybrid Courses**: Look in the course site for information on what technology is used in real time, and what the technology is used for. Examples include breakout rooms for synchronous discussions, Virtual Reality (VR) software used in the face-to-face portion of a hybrid course, or Augmented Reality (AR) software and hardware used to learn complex structures (e.g., examining different layers of a human cadaver).   
* **Competency-Based Courses**: N/A  
* **Synchronous Courses**: Look in the course site for information on what technology is used in real time, and what the technology is used for. Examples include breakout rooms for synchronous discussions, real-time whiteboards, or live polling tools used to support learning objectives during synchronous sessions.   
* **Aligned Elements**:  
  * **Pedagogically Aligned Tool Selection**:  
    * Tools explicitly selected to match the cognitive actions defined in outcomes (e.g., using a diagramming tool to illustrate comparing and contrasting two historical periods).  
    * Simulation software and virtual practice tools used to demonstrate procedural skills (e.g., using a CPR simulation tool on a virtual patient to fulfill a module outcome).  
    * Clear information and instructions provided within learning activities explaining how selected tools (e.g., discussion boards, blogs, whiteboards) directly support learning objectives.  
  * **Diverse Instructional & Interactive Tools**:  
    * Technology tools—whether internal to the LMS or external—used purposefully for content delivery, learner interaction, or providing feedback (e.g., automated self-check exercises, video repositories, collaboration platforms, assignment and quiz tools).  
    * Immersive technologies (virtual, augmented, or extended reality) integrated to facilitate complex spatial or experiential learning outcomes.  
  * **Real-Time & Synchronous Alignment**:  
    * Web conferencing, breakout rooms, AR/VR tools, and real-time collaboration platforms used intentionally in synchronous or hybrid components to facilitate real-time discussion, group work, or interactive demonstrations aligned with learning objectives.  
* **Violating Examples**:  
  * A course outcome requires students to diagram and visualize a complex process (e.g., photosynthesis), but the assessment restricts students to describing the process in a text-only essay using basic word processing software.  
  * A course outcome requires students to discuss and debate a topic, but the instructor uses a multiple-choice LMS Test/Exam tool instead of an interactive discussion platform.  
  * Integrating a complex external social media or blogging tool into a module without providing any instructions or explanation as to how it connects to or supports the module's learning objectives.  
  * Requiring students to download or purchase a specialized software application that is never actually utilized in any assignment, activity, or assessment throughout the course.

The selected course tools support active learning through learner-instructor, learner-content, and learner-learner interaction. Active learning tools that encourage learning by interacting, retrieving, discussing, investigating, and creating are evident. While specific tools are not required for this Specific Review Standard to be met, look for tools that support learner engagement and active learning, such as social media, mobile technologies, games, simulations, blogs, podcasts, polling software, survey tools, and virtual worlds. * **Rationales**: Tools used in the course help learners actively engage in the learning process rather than passively absorb information. Supporting active learning through learner-instructor, learner-content, and learner-learner interaction encourages learners to learn by interacting, retrieving, discussing, investigating, and creating, which enhances retention, fosters community, and deepens conceptual understanding.   
* **Note to Reviewers**: Look for tools that support learner engagement and active learning rather than passive consumption of content. Confirm that course tools facilitate active interaction across three key modes: learner-instructor, learner-content, and learner-learner. Evaluate whether tools encourage learning through interacting, retrieving, discussing, investigating, and creating. Check the Course Worksheet to determine whether learners have bandwidth limitations that impact tool selection.   
* **Hybrid Courses**: Examples of tools that support learners in real-time interaction include web-conferencing software, breakout rooms, simulations, virtual reality technology, and others. Look for a statement in the course that describes the technology learners will use to interact in real time.   
* **Competency-Based Courses**: N/A  
* **Synchronous Courses**: Examples of tools that support learners in real-time interaction include web-conferencing software, breakout rooms, simulations, virtual reality technology, and others. Look for a statement in the course that describes the technology learners will use to interact in real time.   
* **Aligned Elements**:  
  * **Real-Time Synchronous Interaction Tools**:

  * Discussion forums are designed strictly as single-post dropboxes where learners cannot read, reply to, or engage with classmates or the instructor.

* **Note to Reviewers**: Look for links to privacy policies and measures taken to protect learner data. Verify that privacy policy links or statements of non-existence are provided for all instructor-selected tools, publisher resources, and external platforms where learners must log in or create an account. Check for an institutional privacy statement covering vetted LMS tools, privacy details for online proctoring software, and guidance on safeguarding learner data during public or interactive learning activities.

* **Category**: Learner Support  
* **General Standard Summary**: The course facilitates learner access to institutional support services essential to learner success.  
* **General Standard Overview**: It is important to ensure online learners know they have access to and are encouraged to use the services that support learners at the institution. ## **Standard 7.5**

* **ID**: GO-S7.5  
* **Category**: Learner Support  
* **Summary**: Learners are given the opportunity to provide descriptive feedback on course design, course content, course experience, and ease of online technology.  
* **Rubric Type**: Gold  
* **Points**: 2pts  
* **Priority Weight**: Medium  
* **Scope of Implementation**: Course orientation / "Start Here" module, mid-course survey modules, ongoing feedback discussion forums, lesson modules, synchronous live session agendas, and end-of-course feedback modules.  
* **Passing Threshold**: Minimum 1 instance of an explicit channel or opportunity for learners to provide descriptive feedback on course design, content, experience, or technology (ideally provided as an in-progress or ongoing mechanism independent of standard administrative end-of-course surveys).  
* **Annotation**: Online learners have firsthand experience in online learning environments as learners and users of the online learning technologies. They are immersed within the online experience and can provide useful feedback on their experiences with the online course design and delivery. This feedback can be used to guide continuous improvements to the course design and delivery practices and improve the efficacy of the online teaching and learning process. Providing a channel for feedback, and encouraging dialogue among learners, can lead to the improvement of ideas and opinions (Mabrito & Medley, 2008). Learners may find navigation difficult, or content lacking, which can get in the way of successful course completion. Creating mechanisms for learners to provide feedback to the instructor and/or course designer on navigation, access, and the overall learning experience, can guide improvements to support learner success while the course is in progress, as well as at the end of the course. In addition, if new technologies, LMS features, or pedagogical approaches are incorporated into the learning environment, learners need a way to report or explain any issues that arise, offer their insights and suggestions, and share solutions that they may have found that can be shared with their classmates. Independent from end-of-course surveys, providing channels to collect learner feedback on the online learning experience empowers the learner to have a stake in making the experience better for themselves, as well as for other learners in the future. These channels also enhance group cohesion by exemplifying how instructors value the opinions of their learners.   
* **Rationales**: Providing dedicated channels for descriptive learner feedback empowers students to take an active stake in their learning environment and enhances group cohesion by demonstrating that instructors value their perspectives. Because online learners directly experience the course technologies, navigation pathways, and instructional materials, their feedback provides actionable insights for continuous course improvement. Gathering feedback while the course is actively in progress allows instructors to address technical issues, clarify content, and refine navigation in real time, directly supporting student retention and learning efficacy.   
* **Note to Reviewers**: Reviewers should look for explicit mechanisms or planned opportunities where learners can share descriptive feedback about course design, content clarity, ease of technology use, and overall learning experience. Determine whether feedback collection opportunities exist during the course (e.g., mid-course surveys, ongoing feedback discussion boards, sporadic check-ins) independent of or in addition to final end-of-course evaluations. If feedback collection is scheduled during a synchronous live session, verify that it is explicitly noted in the session agenda.   
* **Hybrid Courses**: N/A  
* **Competency-Based Courses**: N/A  
* **Synchronous Courses**: For synchronous sessions, if feedback collection occurs during a live session, it must be specifically documented and noted in the live session agenda.   
* **Aligned Elements**:  
  * **Dedicated Course Design & Experience Surveys**:  
    * Provision of an InEd course design feedback survey or specialized feedback form soliciting input on course structure, content clarity, and technology ease.  
  * **Ongoing & In-Progress Feedback Opportunities**:  
    * Ongoing feedback channels integrated throughout the course, such as sporadic check-in surveys, mid-semester evaluations, or dedicated course feedback discussion forums.  
    * Formative feedback opportunities planned while the course is in progress to enable real-time adjustments to course navigation and learning support.  
  * **Technology & Innovation Reporting Channels**:  
    * Established mechanisms for learners to report issues, explain challenges, offer suggestions, or share student-driven solutions when new technologies, LMS features, or pedagogical approaches are introduced.  
  * **Synchronous Live Session Agenda Integration**:  
    * Feedback collection activities explicitly listed and integrated into the formal agenda for synchronous live sessions.  
* **Violating Examples**:  
  * The course relies solely on an automated institutional end-of-course evaluation survey sent after final grades are submitted, offering no opportunity for students to provide feedback during the term.  
  * A new third-party software application or LMS tool is required for course assignments, but no forum, survey, or feedback mechanism is provided for students to report technical difficulties or usability insights.  
  * The instructor verbally invites feedback during a live lecture, but no written feedback mechanism, survey link, or session agenda item is documented anywhere in the course site.

Readability includes content presentation as well as clarity and legibility of text. This Specific Review Standard is met if course content is clearly presented and organized in a manner that is easy to read and follow.   
* **Rationales**: Course design elements maximize usability by facilitating readability and minimizing distractions. When content is clearly presented, logically organized, and visually legible, learners can easily follow materials, absorb information, and complete learning tasks without experiencing cognitive fatigue or technical friction caused by poor visual formatting.   
* **Note to Reviewers**: Reviewers, determine if the content can be easily read. Evaluate both content organization/presentation (grouping of similar content, consistent styles, white space, standardized instruction headers, and uniform naming conventions) and text legibility (minimal errors, simple fonts, high color contrast, purposeful formatting, and reserving underlining strictly for hyperlinks).

* **Competency-Based Courses**: N/A  
* **Synchronous Courses**: N/A  
* **Aligned Elements**:  
  * **Content Organization & Presentation**:  
    * Grouping of similar content together logically across modules and pages.  
    * Consistent heading and body font styles maintained throughout the course.  
    * Effective use of white space or negative space around content, images, and embedded media to increase comprehension and reduce eye fatigue caused by large blocks of text.  
    * Standardized presentation of learning activity and assessment instructions using consistent headers (e.g., "Instructions," "Grading Information," and "Submission Instructions").

  * Inconsistent naming conventions across course pages, such as referring to the same assessment as "Project 1," "Assignment \#1," and "Paper 1."  
  * Underlining plain text for emphasis, causing learners to confuse non-clickable body text with active hyperlinks.  
  * Low contrast between body text and background color, making content difficult or exhausting to read on screen.

## **Standard 8.3**

* **ID**: GR-S8.3  
* **Category**: Accessibility and Usability  
* **Summary**: Text in the course is accessible.  
* **Rubric Type**: Green  
* **Points**: 3pts  
* **Priority Weight**: High  
* **Scope of Implementation**: Course-wide, across all files, uploaded documents, slide decks, LMS pages, and external websites integrated into the course.  
* **Passing Threshold**: 85% of total examined text present in the course must meet the review criteria.  
* **Annotation**: Text may be found in files, documents, slides, learning management system (LMS) pages, and websites. Reviewers can use accessibility tools and checkers to assist in the review of the accessibility of the text. Accessible text ensures content is discoverable as actual text (rather than images of text), formatted with true structural headings, presented without relying solely on color to convey meaning, structured in accessible data tables, and formatted in selectable, searchable documents (such as searchable PDFs).   
* **Rationales**: When the text in a course is accessible, learners benefit. For example, any learner can search an accessible PDF for keywords. Most learners can access content, complete activities, and interact with others without the need for accommodations.   
* **Note to Reviewers**: When the text in a course is accessible, learners benefit. For example, any learner can search an accessible PDF for keywords. Most learners can access content, complete activities, and interact with others without the need for accommodations.   
* **Hybrid Courses**: N/A  
* **Competency-Based Courses**: N/A  
* **Synchronous Courses**: N/A  
* **Aligned Elements**:  
  * **Discoverable & Selectable Text**:  
    * Text discoverable and selectable as true digital text across files, documents, slides, LMS pages, and websites rather than flattened inside image files. (Images that have text are reviewed with Specific Review Standard 8.4.)  
  * **Hierarchical Heading Structure**:  
    * Native heading styles (Heading 1, Heading 2, etc.) used in a logical order to communicate the structural hierarchy of material on a page or document, rather than merely using visual formatting (e.g., larger, bold, or italic font) to indicate content importance or section separation.  
  * **Color-Independent Meaning**:  
    * Information and instructions designed so text color alone is not relied on to convey meaning, ensuring meaning is also conveyed in another way that does not require perceiving different colors.  
  * **Accessible Data Table Structure**:  
    * Tables set up as true text elements rather than embedded image screen captures, allowing individual cells to be selected and navigated sequentially from left to right using the Tab key.  
    * Tables configured with designated headings for columns and rows, repeating headers, and captions, alt-text, or alt attribute tags providing context.  
    * Tables used exclusively for summarizing data (not for page layout or formatting), strictly avoiding merged cells so cells match relevant row and column headers.  
  * **Searchable PDFs & Form Navigation**:  
    * PDF files containing text that are not image scans, ensuring any text within the document is selectable and searchable.  
    * Fillable PDF forms formatted so the cursor moves in a logical order when navigating with the Tab key.  
* **Violating Examples**:  
  * An uploaded reading assignment provided as an image-only scanned PDF where text cannot be highlighted, selected, or searched for keywords.  
  * An LMS module page that uses large, bolded, red body text to simulate section headings instead of using native structural heading tags (H1, H2, H3).  
  * A course assignment page instructing students to "complete only the red-colored items" in a list without offering text labels or secondary symbols for colorblind learners.  
  * A data table embedded as a static screenshot image file, preventing screen readers from reading row/column headers or tabbing through individual data cells.  
  * A complex data table featuring merged cells, missing row/column header designations, and no table caption or descriptive alt attribute tags.

## **Standard 8.4**

* **ID**: GR-S8.4  
* **Category**: Accessibility and Usability  
* **Summary**: Images in the course are accessible.  
* **Rubric Type**: Green  
* **Points**: 3pts  
* **Priority Weight**: High  
* **Scope of Implementation**: Course-wide, across all LMS pages, web pages, uploaded documents, narrated slide presentations, and assignments where images (photographs, charts, graphs, infographics, maps, etc.) are present.   
* **Passing Threshold:** 85% of total examined images present in the course must meet the review criteria.    
* **Annotation**: Images that are meaningful to the learning experience include alternative text (alt-text), long descriptions, or other means of description. Images that are decorative are marked as such. Images can be found in narrated slide presentations, web pages, documents, and within the LMS pages. Images include photographs, charts, graphs, infographics, maps, etc.    
* **Rationales**: When images in a course are accessible, most learners benefit. For example, alt-text can provide a description if the image cannot load due to limited internet bandwidth, or long descriptions and figure captions can guide learners through a complex visual, ensuring all learners have equivalent access to the instructional material.    
* **Note to Reviewers**: Verify the accuracy of the alternate content provided. Verification is important, as not all attempts to provide alternative formats meet the goal of providing equivalent access for diverse learners. For example, if the alt-text does not include the equivalent textual information presented in an image, some learners will not have equivalent access to the material. Check object properties to confirm alt-text, look for two sets of quotation marks with no content between them ("") to indicate decorative ("null") alt-text, look for visible figure captions on the page or document, and look for long descriptions embedded in HTML or provided below the content with a descriptive link (e.g., "Detailed description of image"). The Specific Review Standard is met if 85% of the images are accessible. Content developed by publishers is included in the review of this Specific Review Standard, but accessibility cannot be ensured in all cases.    
* **Hybrid Courses**: N/A   
* **Competency-Based Courses**: N/A   
* **Synchronous Courses**: N/A  
* **Aligned Elements**:  
  * **Infographic Alternative Text**:  
    * An infographic featuring alt-text that describes its basic structural components, moving logically from general concepts to detailed information (verified in object properties).  
  * **Map Descriptions**:  
    * A map containing alt-text that describes its key elements, such as the overall purpose, historical timeframe, and directional information.  
  * **Decorative Image Marking (Null Alt-Text)**:  
    * Decorative border images, background accents, or decorative elements in assignment descriptions indicating in the alt-text that they are decorative so screen readers bypass them (using null alt-text, indicated by alt="").  
  * **Visible Figure Captions for Diagrams**:  
    * Complex diagrams (e.g., a circuit diagram) described in a visible figure caption directly on the page or document, guiding the learner through the sequential pathways or components.  
  * **Long Descriptions for Complex Visual Data**:  
    * A line graph or complex data display accompanied by a long description specifying the title, x- and y-axis labels, numerical ranges, and a summary of trend lines.  
    * Long descriptions embedded directly in HTML or provided below the content with a clear, descriptive link (e.g., "Detailed description of image").  
  * **Multi-Format Image Accessibility**:  
    * Meaningful images across all course materials—including LMS web pages, uploaded documents, and narrated slide presentations—equipped with accurate alt-text, figure captions, or long descriptions.  
* **Violating Examples**:  
  * A complex line graph displaying economic trends across multiple decades that lacks alt-text, figure captions, or a long description, offering only a raw file name like graph\_v2.png.  
  * An infographic outlining a six-step research methodology that uses brief alt-text reading simply "Research infographic," omitting the actual text and steps depicted in the graphic.  
  * Decorative page borders and visual divider graphics on an assignment page missing null alt-text (alt=""), causing screen readers to read out long, non-descriptive file paths.  
  * A scientific circuit diagram embedded on an LMS page without a visible figure caption or surrounding text description explaining the path and components of the circuit.  
  * A course where more than 15% of images across uploaded documents, slide decks, and LMS pages lack alternative text or decorative tags entirely.

* **ID**: GR-S8.6

* **Synchronous Courses**: N/A

  * **Media Player Controls & Functionality**:  
    * Audio and video players equipped with complete user controls, including play, pause, fast-forward, rewind, and volume adjustments.

* **Hybrid Courses**: N/A  
* **Competency-Based Courses**: N/A  
* **Synchronous Courses**: N/A  
* **Aligned Elements**:  
  * **Comprehensive Vendor Accessibility Documentation & Tool Coverage**:  
    * Direct links to official vendor accessibility statements or Voluntary Product Accessibility Templates (VPATs) provided for the primary Learning Management System (LMS) and all integrated third-party software.  
    * Accessibility statements provided for digital content platforms, including publisher materials, digital textbooks, document-sharing systems, and slide presentation software.

    * Accessibility statement links prominently hosted within foundational course navigation areas, such as the course syllabus, a dedicated "Required Technology" page, or a course resources module.

* **Violating Examples**:  
  * A course requires students to use an external interactive polling tool and a publisher platform, but provides no links to the vendor accessibility statements for either technology.  
  * An external video annotation tool used in the course lacks an official accessibility statement, but the course completely omits any disclosure or vendor contact information regarding its accessibility status.  
  * The syllabus lists five required software tools and mobile apps but only includes an accessibility link for the LMS, omitting the remaining four required technologies.
